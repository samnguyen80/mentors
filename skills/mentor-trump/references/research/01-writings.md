### Main books Trump has published

| Year | Title | Co-author | Nature |
|------|-------|-----------|--------|
| 1987 | *Trump: The Art of the Deal* | Tony Schwartz (ghostwriter) | Business memoir + negotiation philosophy |
| 1990 | *Trump: Surviving at the Top* | Charles Leerhsen | Sequel |

| 2006 | *Trump 101: The Way to Success* | Meredith McIver | Self-help |
| 2007 | *Think Big and Kick Ass in Business and Life* | Bill Zanker | Motivation / drive |
| 2008 | *Why We Want You to Be Rich* | Robert Kiyosaki | Co-authored wealth education |
| 2015 | *Crippled America: How to Make America Great Again* | Sole author | Campaign political manifesto |

**Important caveat (credibility issue)**: *The Art of the Deal* was actually written by ghostwriter Tony Schwartz. Schwartz stated: "Trump didn't write so much as a postcard to us." (Source: publisher Howard Kaminsky.) Trump himself has contradicted himself, first saying "I chose Schwartz to write this book," then later saying "Schwartz didn't write it, I did." ★★★ (Primary source: Schwartz's interviews with The New Yorker/NPR, 2016)

The principles in *The Art of the Deal* therefore reflect **Schwartz's observations of Trump**, not Trump's own pen. However, the stories and business logic in the book were confirmed by Trump himself and represent his public self-narrative.

## 2. *The Art of the Deal* (1987): The 11 Negotiation Principles

> Source: the summary of principles that follows the first chapter, "Dealing: A Week in the Life of Donald Trump"
> Credibility: ★★ (ghostwritten by Schwartz, but the principles were endorsed by Trump and are repeatedly cited throughout his political career)

### The 11 principles in full

**1. Think Big**
Don't set small goals. Trump believes most people limit their goals because they fear big decisions, which is essentially a fear of success. He describes his own drive as "controlled neurosis": an obsessive focus on the direction of his work.
> Original text: "I like thinking big. I always have. To me it's very simple: if you're going to be thinking anyway, you might as well think big."

**2. Protect the Downside / Prepare for the Worst**
Success is not about one big gamble; it is about structurally making sure failure is never fatal. In negotiations he always leaves himself an exit, asks for guarantees, and builds in exit clauses. He advances incrementally to avoid catastrophic losses.
> Logic: most deals will fail, so make every failure survivable.

**3. Maintain Flexibility / Have a Backup Plan**
Never bet on a single hand. Keep a Plan B, C, and D, and don't become overly attached to any single outcome. When Trump's New York residential development was blocked, he turned to developing the convention center, which bears out this principle.

**4. Know Your Market / Know Your Audience**
He does not rely on market research or consultants' reports; he relies on **intuition and intelligence gathered directly from real people**. "I trust my own judgment over any survey." His grasp of audience psychology is instinctive, not data-driven.

**5. Use Leverage**
Never look desperate. Position yourself as the solution to someone else's problem, not as the party in need.
> Original text (from the book): "My style of deal-making is quite simple and straightforward. I aim very high, and then I just keep pushing and pushing and pushing to get what I'm after."

**6. Enhance Location Through Deal Structure**
He challenges the traditional real-estate rule of "location, location, location." The point is not to find the perfect location, but to create value in an ordinary location through creative deal structure. Marketing and perception can change reality.

**7. Leverage Media / Promote**
Free media coverage is cheaper and more credible than advertising. Core concept: **"Truthful Hyperbole"**, a harmless exaggeration that captures attention and drives business.
> Original text: "I play to people's fantasies. People may not always think big themselves, but they can still get very excited by those who do... I call it truthful hyperbole. It's an innocent form of exaggeration—and it's a very effective form of promotion."

**8. Fight Back If You Think You're Right**
Success invites envy and attack. Hold your position even if it offends people. Giving up the fight is worse than living with regret; even if you ultimately lose, at least you know you used every option.

**9. Deliver Real Value / Have a Good Product**
Marketing cannot replace substance forever. In the short term you can get by on promotion; in the long term you depend on real delivery. Building credit depends on **keeping your promises**.

**10. Keep Costs Low / Contain Costs**
Even on a project worth millions of dollars, negotiate every penny. Small amounts add up, so control spending strictly.

**11. Enjoy the Ride / Have Fun**
Negotiation is itself a game, and enjoying the process matters more than caring about material results. Focus on the present; don't dwell on the past or the future.

### The paradox of negotiation

The book contains a self-contradiction: Trump writes that offering negotiation techniques at all goes against one of his own principles: "great negotiators are born, not made." He believes negotiation depends mainly on instinct, not on techniques that can be learned. ★★★

## 3. The Definition of and Obsession with "Winning"

> Source: across several books + speeches + biographical research
> Credibility: ★★★ (primary quotations) + ★★ (biographical research)

### Trump's core definition of "winning"

**Winning is personal validation, not just material results**

Campaign slogan: "We're going to win so much, you're going to be so sick and tired of winning." (2016 campaign rally) ★★★

In his political narrative, "winning" and "losing" are the basic frame for understanding America's situation:
- "America doesn't win anymore. We lose to China on trade, we lose to our enemies in the military, we lose to every country in diplomacy." (campaign speeches, recurring) ★★★
- "Success cannot be measured by money alone; it must also be measured by happiness." (*The Art of the Comeback*, 1997) ★★★

### Psychological perspective (secondary)

Biographer Michael D'Antonio (*Never Enough*, 2015) argues that Trump's brand-building method rests on a firm belief that "he is a singular and superior human being." He admits to avoiding self-reflection "because I might not like what I see," and believes "most people are not worthy of respect." ★★

Psychological analysis: the concept of "the episodic man." Trump lacks a coherent life narrative and lives episode by episode, needing to win the battle of the moment each time. "For Trump, the truth is whatever helps win in the moment." ★ (secondary inference)

### The father's influence

Fred Trump's approach to upbringing was extremely transactional: success was rewarded, failure was ignored or punished. He once told Donald: "You are a king, but a king must work harder than anyone to prove his worth." ★★ (D'Antonio biography)

### "Winning" themes repeated across books (appearing 3 or more times)

| Theme | Where it recurs |
|-------|-----------------|
| America is losing on trade | *Crippled America*, 2016 speeches, inaugural address |
| We elected stupid negotiators | *Crippled America*, campaign rallies, Twitter |
| Winning requires toughness; the weak get exploited | *Art of the Deal*, *Art of the Comeback*, political speeches |
| Revenge is a necessary part of success | *Think Big and Kick Ass*, *Art of the Comeback* |

## 4. Theory of Media and Information

> Source: *The Art of the Deal* (1987), *Think Big and Kick Ass* (2007), campaign speeches
> Credibility: ★★★ (direct quotations from the books)

### 4.1 The "Truthful Hyperbole" theory

This is **one of the only concepts Trump personally named** in *The Art of the Deal*:

> "I call it truthful hyperbole. It's an innocent form of exaggeration—and it's a very effective form of promotion."

Logic: people want to believe something is the biggest, the best, the most spectacular. If you give them that fantasy, you are serving them. It is not a lie; it is a form of "innocent exaggeration."

### 4.2 The media is a tool that can be played

Trump has said publicly many times how he "plays the press":
- The media needs good stories, and you can make a story better by adding exaggeration and controversy
- Bad publicity can also bring attention; the key is how to turn it into a business opportunity
- Asked how he stays well known without spending on advertising, Trump answered: "You make news."

Key observation: "The press takes him literally, but not seriously; his supporters take him seriously, but not literally." This distinction shows how "Truthful Hyperbole" lands differently with different audiences in a political context. ★★

### 4.3 The politicization of "Fake News" (after 2016)

In his political career Trump turned "Fake News" from a descriptive term into an **offensive weapon**:
- At first it was used to criticize media that had reported incorrectly about him
- It gradually expanded into a label for any media report critical of him
- Its actual function: **to discredit in advance any information source that is unfavorable to him**

This is a theoretical continuation of the media strategy in *The Art of the Deal*: you need to control the narrative and not be defined by others.

### 4.4 Record of contradiction

In 1987 Trump actively maintained relationships with reporters and made heavy use of the media; in his political career he described the media as "the enemy of the people."

Interpretations (recorded only, not reconciled):
- Path A: he has always been "using" the media, and the stance itself is the strategy
- Path B: in 1987 the media was his ally and after 2016 it became his adversary; the stance flips with his interests

## 5. The Underlying Logic of America First

> Source: *Crippled America* (2015), April 2016 foreign policy speech, 2017 inaugural address (official archive)
> Credibility: ★★★ (primary sources)

### 5.1 Structure of the core argument

Trump's "America First" is not isolationism but **a global application of business-negotiation thinking**:

1. **Diagnosis**: America is run by bad negotiators who have signed a string of terrible deals
2. **Attribution**: career politicians, special interest groups, globalist elites
3. **Remedy**: replace diplomatic convention with business-negotiation principles: demand a hard price, make mutually beneficial exchanges, and don't pay for values

Direct quotation from the inaugural address: "a small group in our nation's Capital has reaped the rewards of government while the people have borne the cost." ★★★

### 5.2 The zero-sum view of trade

Trump's view of trade is a standard **zero-sum game**: trade deficit = America is losing; other countries sell more than they buy = America is being taken advantage of; tariffs = a weapon for tit-for-tat.

Direct quotation (2024): "To me, the most beautiful word in the dictionary is tariff, and it's my favourite word." ★★★

Historical argument (recurring): "From 1789 to 1913 we were a tariff-backed nation, and the United States was proportionately the wealthiest it has ever been." ★★★
(Note: this is his historical interpretation, and economists assess it differently; only his argument is recorded here.)

### 5.3 The national narrative framework

A three-layer narrative in the inaugural address:
- Globalization "redistributed" wealth from the American middle class "to the entire world"
- "The forgotten men and women" are the moral basis for policy reform
- "This American carnage stops right here and stops right now" (his characterization of the state of America at the time) ★★★

This narrative directly grafts on the "leverage" concept from *The Art of the Deal*: America has lost its bargaining chips and needs a tough leader to win them back.

### 5.4 National interest before values

April 2016 foreign policy speech (primary text):

> "No country has ever prospered that failed to put its own interests first. We will no longer surrender this country or its people to the false song of globalism. The nation-state remains the true foundation for happiness and harmony." ★★★

Academic analysis summarizes five features of Trumpist foreign policy (★★, secondary):
- A preference for bilateral over multilateral arrangements
- Pursuit of near-term economic gains over lasting commitments
- A zero-sum view of international relations
- Indifference to the political systems of partner countries
- No grand strategic vision

## 6. Transferring Business Thinking to Political Decision-Making

> Source: *Crippled America* (2015), academic analysis literature
> Credibility: ★★ (composite analysis)

### 6.1 Trump's self-positioning: the businessman president

Core argument of *Crippled America*:
- Career politicians fail because they play politics as politics
- America's problems are essentially management problems
- A successful business negotiator can solve problems that government cannot
- "Outsider" status is an asset, not a liability

The logic in *Crippled America*: "no business of any size should pay more than 15% in taxes", which treats tax reform as a corporate pricing problem. ★★★ (direct quotation from the book)

### 6.2 Business principles transferred directly

| Business principle (from Art of the Deal) | Political application |
|-------------------------------------------|-----------------------|
| Ask high and leave room | Tariff negotiations: open with an extremely high price, then "concede" to the target level |
| Use leverage and never look desperate | Diplomatic negotiations: threaten to leave alliances and renegotiate terms |
| Always fight back | Attacks on and retaliation against critics |
| Any publicity is good publicity | Controversial statements as media strategy |
| Control the narrative | The "Fake News" label pre-emptively discredits opponents |

### 6.3 The fundamental tension between business logic and political logic (recorded contradiction)

Scholars point to fundamental differences:
- A business deal can be walked away from; a country cannot truly exit geopolitics
- Business counterparties can be replaced; alliances carry accumulated history and the cost of trust
- After a deal closes, the relationship can be settled; international relations are a continuing game

Trump's business logic works in some settings (bilateral trade negotiations) and creates systematic friction in others (multilateral alliances, values-based diplomacy). ★

## 8. Reading List and Intellectual Lineage

> Source: books Trump mentioned in his books and interviews
> Credibility: ★★ (public statements; the true extent of his reading is hard to verify)

### Books Trump says he has read or recommends

- *The Art of the Deal* / *Surviving at the Top* (his own books, listed as favorites)
- *The Prince* by Machiavelli, recommended in *Trump 101*
- *The Art of War* by Sun Tzu, recommended in *Trump 101*
- Collected works of Ralph Waldo Emerson, reportedly a favorite in his early years
- *All Quiet on the Western Front*, which he called "one of the greatest books of all time"
- Several conservative political books attacking Obama and Hillary (written by Fox News hosts and others)

Observation: many of Trump's recommended books are books about himself, books criticizing his opponents, and classics emphasizing power and victory (Machiavelli, Sun Tzu). ★★

### Core intellectual lineage: two key figures

**Norman Vincent Peale (author of *The Power of Positive Thinking*)**
- From childhood, Trump attended services at Peale's Marble Collegiate Church
- Peale's core teaching: think only of the best outcome, "accentuate the positive, eliminate the negative"
- Trump says Peale regarded him as "the greatest student he ever had" (not independently confirmed) ★
- Influence on Trump: staying confident in adversity; reframing real problems as challenges that can be beaten
- Source: CNBC (2020), multiple biographical studies ★★★

**Roy Cohn (lawyer, counsel to the McCarthy-era investigating committee)**
- Trump's lawyer and mentor (1973-1986)
- Cohn's style: no compromise, hit back at all criticism, destroy opponents
- Biographers hold that the principle of never admitting error and always hitting back came directly from Cohn
- Source: biographical research ★★

## 9. *The Art of the Comeback* (1997): Supplementary Points

> Source: original text of the book (via ontheissues.org)
> Credibility: ★★★ (primary source)

- "I am a firm believer in learning from adversity. The worst of times can often turn into your advantage. My life has been a study of this."
- "I'm a little paranoid. I've noticed over the years that people who are a little paranoid tend to be the most successful. Let a little paranoia rule you!"
- "Passion is a key ingredient of success and of coming back."
- Self-diagnosis of the cause of failure: arrogance and complacency, "I was cocky and lazy," his explanation for the 1990 bankruptcy
- "Contracts are very important in business and in personal life" (implication: don't trust verbal promises)
- **The ten comeback principles explicitly include "Get Even"**, one of the success strategies the book recommends

> Credibility: ★★★ (textual evidence exists)

| Area | Position A | Position B |
|------|------------|------------|
| Iraq War | Supported it in interviews before 2003 | Claimed during the campaign to have always opposed it |
| Syrian refugees | In one 2015 interview: humanitarian considerations mean they should be admitted | The next day: they cannot be admitted |
| Abortion | In early years described himself as pro-choice | Supported restrictions once in office |
| Attitude toward the media | 1987: actively used the media and maintained relationships | After 2016: "enemy of the people" |
| Debt | Used heavy leveraged borrowing in his personal business | Criticizes national debt politically |
| Authorship of *The Art of the Deal* | "I chose Schwartz to write it" | "Schwartz didn't write it, I did" |
| Protectionism | Used foreign steel in his own business (documented) | Demands American steel as policy |

**Markers of stable "true belief"** (repeated 3 or more times across time, not changing with interest):
- America has been ruined by bad negotiators
- A trade deficit is proof of losing
