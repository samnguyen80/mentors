## I. Views from Colleagues and Collaborators

### 1.1 Jony Ive (Chief Design Officer, 1992-2019)

**Core assessment**: Jobs was the most excellence-focused person he had ever met, but that focus came at an enormous cost.

- "It's important to remember, you could have had somebody who didn't ever argue, but you wouldn't have the phones that you have now, and a whole list of other things." — Ive defending Jobs's conflict-driven management, arguing that arguing was the necessary price of creating excellence
- "The appetite to learn... to learn is more important than being right." — Ive holds that curiosity (not stubbornness) was the trait that defined Jobs
- Tim Cook said Isaacson's biography portrayed Jobs as "a greedy, selfish egomaniac"; Ive was also unhappy with it and instead endorsed Schlender's *Becoming Steve Jobs*

**Credibility**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Ive worked with Jobs for nearly 20 years and was his most central creative partner)
**Bias**: Clearly favorable, but Ive was the only person who could argue with Jobs on design, so his assessment carries the weight of firsthand experience

**Source**: [Tim Cook, Jony Ive, and Laurene Powell Jobs interview - 9to5Mac](https://9to5mac.com/2022/09/07/tim-cook-steve-jobs-jony-ive-laurene-jobs-interview/)

### 1.2 Tim Cook (CEO successor, 2011 to present)

**Core assessment**: Jobs was a once-in-a-millennium person, but his core legacy is "doing the right thing," not imitating him.

- "I saw in Steve something I'd never seen in a CEO before. He is a once-in-a-thousand-years kind of person."
- Jobs's leadership advice to Cook: "Never ask what I would do. Just do the right thing." — this line itself reveals Jobs's wariness of self-worship
- Cook publicly criticized Isaacson's portrayal of Jobs, but also acknowledged that Jobs did have a harsh side

**Credibility**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Cook is the successor and has a motive to protect Jobs's legacy, but his statements are consistent with his actions)

**Source**: [The Unexpected Lesson Tim Cook Says Steve Jobs Taught Him - Inc.](https://www.inc.com/jason-aten/the-lesson-tim-cook-says-steve-jobs-taught-him-about-leadership/90995373)

### 1.3 Steve Wozniak (co-founder)

**Core assessment**: Jobs was not an engineer, but he was the one who turned technology into products.

- "Steve didn't ever code. He wasn't an engineer and he didn't do any original design." — Wozniak repeatedly made clear that Jobs's role was non-technical
- "Wozniak's genius wasn't enough to keep Apple thriving without Jobs' visionary leadership." — admitting that his own technical genius was not enough to sustain the company
- Their relationship was complicated: early on Jobs treated Wozniak unfairly in dividing up money (the Atari Breakout incident), but Wozniak never publicly turned hostile toward Jobs

**Credibility**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (the earliest partner, who knew both Jobs's talent and had experienced his unfairness)

**Source**: [Apple Turns 50: Wozniak and Tim Cook Reflect - Parade](https://parade.com/news/apple-turns-50-steve-wozniak-and-tim-cook-reflect-on-special-moments-with-steve-jobs)

### 1.4 Andy Hertzfeld (core engineer on the original Mac team)

**Core assessment**: Jobs's main contribution was his passion for excellence, and making the team believe they were doing something great.

- "Steve's main contribution was his passion for excellence. He's a perfectionist. Good enough isn't good enough."
- "Part of Steve's job was to drum into us how important what we were doing actually would be to the world." — Jobs's role was to give the work meaning
- Hertzfeld was an early popularizer of the "reality distortion field" concept; he criticized Jobs's manipulativeness while also acknowledging that this manipulation produced incredible results
- "A lot of people thought Steve Jobs was a jerk... He was complicated."

**Credibility**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (a firsthand witness with a relatively neutral stance, both praising and criticizing)

**Source**: [Andy Hertzfeld Quotes - BrainyQuote](https://www.brainyquote.com/authors/andy-hertzfeld-quotes); [Andy Hertzfeld on the Macintosh - Medium](https://daniellenewnham.medium.com/how-the-macintosh-was-made-interview-with-andy-hertzfeld-fa09cb54d97c)

### 1.5 Scott Forstall (head of iOS, later fired)

**Core assessment**: Jobs acted with shocking decisiveness.

- Forstall recalled his first interview: "Jobs barged in, peppered questions, and concluded, 'We're giving you an offer… Pretend you care in the interviews.'" — Jobs skipped the process entirely and made personnel decisions on instinct
- Forstall was regarded as the person inside Apple most like Jobs (including political skill and product instinct), and for that reason clashed with other executives after Jobs's death and was ultimately fired by Cook

**Credibility**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (a firsthand witness, but the experience of being fired may make his recollections selective)

**Source**: [MacRumors Forums - Scott Forstall interview story](https://forums.macrumors.com/threads/former-ios-chief-scott-forstall-shares-intriguing-story-of-his-interview-with-steve-jobs-at-next.2237665/)

## II. Views from Competitors

### 2.1 Bill Gates (Microsoft co-founder)

**Core assessment**: Jobs had unmatched taste and product instinct, but limited technical knowledge.

- "He really never knew much about technology, but he had an amazing instinct for what works." — Gates's positioning of Jobs: an intuitive, not a technologist
- "I'd give a lot to have Steve's taste." (2007 AllThingsD conference) — a rare public compliment
- "I respect Steve, we got to work together. We spurred each other on, even as competitors." (after Jobs's death)
- Jobs on Gates: "I admire him for the company he built — it's impressive — and I enjoyed working with him. He's bright and actually has a good sense of humor."
- But Jobs also said harsher things: "Bill is basically unimaginative and has never invented anything... He just shamelessly ripped off other people's ideas."

**The nature of their relationship**: from cooperation (Microsoft developing software for the Mac) → fierce competition (the GUI fight) → Gates rescuing Apple (the 1997 $150 million investment) → reconciliation late in life (their last meeting before Jobs died, when the two talked the whole afternoon)

**Core disagreement**:
- Jobs: vertical integration, controlling the full-stack experience (hardware + software + services)
- Gates: horizontal expansion, making software ubiquitous (licensing to all hardware makers)

**Credibility**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (the competitor who knew Jobs best, a relationship spanning 30 years)

**Source**: [Bill Gates talks Steve Jobs in "Source Code" memoir - Axios](https://www.axios.com/2025/02/03/bill-gates-interview-steve-jobs-rivalry); [Steve Jobs and Bill Gates rivalry - Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/video/2017/7/31/steve-jobs-and-bill-gates-inside-the-rivalry)

### 2.2 Michael Dell

**Core assessment**: From mocking Apple to being overtaken by Apple, he became Jobs's favorite target.

- In 1997, asked how he would save Apple, Dell said: "I'd shut it down and give the money back to the shareholders."
- Jobs's reaction: privately told employees "Fuck Michael Dell"; publicly responded at Macworld: "We're coming after you, buddy."
- In 2006, Apple's market cap surpassed Dell's, and Jobs sent a company-wide email: "It turned out that Michael Dell wasn't perfect at predicting the future."
- Dell later tried to clarify, but the story had already become core material in the narrative of Jobs's comeback

**What this story means**: it perfectly captures Jobs's character — holding grudges, competitively driven, and good at turning insults into fuel

**Credibility**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (a public event, verified by multiple parties)

**Source**: [Michael Dell quote on Apple - Cult of Mac](https://www.cultofmac.com/apple-history/michael-dell-quote-about-apple); [Dell speaks out on infamous quote - Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2011-10-18-dell-speaks-out-on-infamous-apple-quote.html)

### 2.3 Eric Schmidt (Google CEO, formerly an Apple board member)

**Core assessment**: Jobs's anger at Android was real; he saw it as a betrayal.

- Schmidt served on Apple's board (2006-2009) and was forced to resign after Google released Android phones
- Jobs told Isaacson: "I will spend my last dying breath if I need to, and I will spend every penny of Apple's $40 billion in the bank, to right this wrong. I'm going to destroy Android, because it's a stolen product."
- This reaction reflects Jobs's extreme sensitivity to "theft" — even though he himself had "borrowed" the GUI from Xerox PARC

**Credibility**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (a primary-source quotation from the Isaacson biography)

**Source**: Walter Isaacson, *Steve Jobs* (2011)

## III. Systematic Criticism

### 3.1 Criticism of management style: tyrannical management and emotional manipulation

**The most common criticism**: Jobs was a boss who made people cry.

Key evidence:
- Mac employee Debi Coleman: "He would shout at a meeting, 'you asshole, you never do anything right!' It was like an hourly occurrence."
- Early Apple investor Mike Markkula: "He became increasingly tyrannical and sharp in his criticism."
- When he could not persuade or coax, Jobs would "get emotional and cry" — crying used as a tool of manipulation
- He would publicly humiliate employees' work, then turn around and present the same idea as his own

**Reality Distortion Field**:
- The concept was coined by Bud Tribble in 1981 and spread widely by Andy Hertzfeld
- Definition: Jobs used a mix of "charm, charisma, bravado, hyperbole, marketing, appeasement and persistence" to make the people around him believe the impossible was possible
- It distorted colleagues' perception of difficulty and scale
- The key paradox: the RDF was both manipulation and a catalyst for achieving the impossible. Many former employees admit in hindsight that without Jobs "distorting reality" they would not have produced what they did

**Academic analysis**:
- The Hogan Assessments paper "The Dark Side of Steve Jobs" notes that Jobs showed "very low interpersonal relationships, low empathy, low impulse control, low flexibility, and low reality testing" on emotional-intelligence assessments
- The High Conflict Institute classifies Jobs as a potential "High Conflict Personality" (HCP)

**Is this criticism valid?** Mostly it is factual. The debate is whether this management style was a necessary condition for creating excellent products. Tim Cook proved that Apple can be run without tyrannical management, but no one can prove that Apple under Cook has reached the heights of product innovation of the Jobs era.

**Credibility**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (cross-validated by multiple independent sources)

**Source**: [The Dark Side of Steve Jobs - Hogan Assessments](https://www.hoganassessments.com/wp-content/uploads/2012/10/Article-The20Dark20Side20of20Steve20Jobs_0.pdf); [Steve Jobs Had Moderately Low EI - Workplace Psychology](https://workplacepsychology.wordpress.com/2020/01/06/steve-jobs-had-moderately-low-emotional-intelligence/); [HCP or Collaborative Leader - High Conflict Institute](https://www.highconflictinstitute.com/lessons-from-steve-jobs-biography-hcp-or-collaborative-leader/)

### 3.2 The dispute over who deserves credit for innovation: innovator or integrator?

**Core dispute**: Jobs himself invented no core technology. Was he an innovator, or merely an excellent integrator/marketer?

**Arguments for "Jobs was just a marketing genius"**:
- Wozniak: "Steve didn't ever code. He wasn't an engineer and he didn't do any original design."
- Daniel Kottke (one of Apple's earliest employees): "Between Woz and Jobs, Woz was the innovator, the inventor."
- The GUI came from Xerox PARC, the mouse from Douglas Engelbart, MP3 players already existed, and touchscreen phones preceded the iPhone (the LG Prada)
- Apple's core technologies were often acquired or borrowed

**Arguments for "Jobs was a true innovator"**:
- Innovation ≠ invention. Jobs's innovation lay in **systems integration**: combining hardware, software, services, design, and retail into a seamless experience
- The iPod's innovation was not the MP3 player but the complete ecosystem of iPod + iTunes + iTunes Store
- The iPhone's innovation was not the touchscreen but the redefinition of multi-touch UI + App Store + the mobile internet
- Jobs innovated business models (the Apple Store, the App Store economy) and ways of organizing (vertical integration)
- HBR's analysis: "Steve Jobs Solved the Innovator's Dilemma" — he solved the problem that large companies cannot disrupt themselves

**A fairer assessment**: Jobs was neither a pure inventor nor merely a marketer. He was a **product architect** who could see the possibilities in technology and integrate scattered elements into complete products that changed user behavior. This ability is extremely rare.

**Credibility**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (every side has facts behind it, and the debate itself is valuable)

**Source**: [Steve Jobs Solved the Innovator's Dilemma - HBR](https://hbr.org/2011/10/steve-jobs-solved-the-innovato); [Innovation lessons from Steve Jobs - Innosight](https://www.innosight.com/insight/innovation-lessons-from-the-life-of-steve-jobs/)

### 3.3 Criticism of the closed ecosystem

**Core criticism**: Apple's closed ecosystem is a prison for consumers.

- In a 2010 internal email Jobs instructed: "Tie all of our products together, so we further lock customers into our ecosystem." — the word "lock" itself exposes the intent
- Jobs initially did not even want third-party apps on the iPhone; it was the team that persuaded him to open the App Store
- "Integrated vs. fragmented" — Jobs redefined closed as "integrated" and open as "fragmented." A classic Jobs-style manipulation of framing

**Critics' arguments**:
- Users are locked into the Apple ecosystem, and switching costs are extremely high
- Developers are constrained by the App Store's 30% commission and strict review
- In 2024 the US DOJ sued Apple on antitrust grounds over its "walled garden" strategy
- Much innovation has been stifled because it did not fit Apple's rules

**Supporters' arguments**:
- Closed means guarantees of security, consistency, and user experience
- Satisfaction and retention in the Apple ecosystem are far higher than competitors'
- Although the App Store charges a lot, it gives developers unified distribution and payment infrastructure

**Jobs's real attitude**: it was not that he didn't know the cost of being closed, but that he believed **control is the precondition of quality**. This is consistent with his overall worldview — he did not trust users to make good choices, nor developers to maintain quality standards.

**Credibility**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (the facts are clear; the dispute is over value judgments)

**Source**: [Apple's Walled Garden targeted in antitrust lawsuit - TechTimes](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/302853/20240322/apple-walled-garden-approach-targeted-new-us-antitrust-lawsuit.htm); [Steve Jobs defends his walled garden - Salon](https://www.salon.com/2010/06/02/steve_jobs_walled_garden/)

### 3.4 Criticism of family relationships

**The most serious personal criticism**: Jobs denied his biological daughter Lisa for years.

Key facts:
- In 1978, 23-year-old Jobs refused to acknowledge paternity after his girlfriend Chrisann Brennan became pregnant
- Even when a DNA test showed a 94.41% probability of paternity (he was later confirmed as the father), Jobs still claimed "28% of the male population of the United States could be the father"
- Lisa and her mother lived on welfare while Jobs was already a millionaire
- Jobs was ultimately compelled by the court to acknowledge paternity and pay child support

Key details from Lisa Brennan-Jobs's memoir *Small Fry* (2018):
- Jobs denied that the Lisa computer was named after her
- Jobs told her "You're getting nothing"
- Jobs once said to her "You smell like a toilet"
- Her childhood was full of emotional coldness and unpredictability

**Later reconciliation**:
- After leaving Apple, Jobs began to accept Lisa
- He apologized for his behavior many times
- Lisa was at his side in Jobs's final days
- Jobs's will left Lisa millions of dollars

**The family's rebuttal**:
- Laurene Powell Jobs (Jobs's widow) stated that *Small Fry* "is not the husband and father we knew"
- "Steve loved Lisa, and he regretted that he was not the father he should have been during her early childhood."

**Is this criticism valid?** Entirely. Jobs's early neglect of Lisa is indefensible. But the story is not simply black and white — he did try to make amends later, and expressed genuine remorse before his death.

**Credibility**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Lisa's own memoir + court records + corroboration from multiple parties)

**Source**: [Lisa Brennan-Jobs CNN interview](https://www.cnn.com/2018/09/12/tech/lisa-brennan-jobs-amanpour); [Steve Jobs' Widow response - TIME](https://time.com/5380968/steve-jobs-daughter-memoir-reaction/); [Steve Jobs denied daughter - Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/steve-jobs-denied-daughter-years-154603931.html)

### 3.5 Other criticisms

**Stock option backdating scandal**:
- In 2006, Apple was exposed for backdating problems in the 7.5 million stock options granted to Jobs, with the exercise price artificially set low ($18.30 vs. the $21.10 it should have been)
- Apple admitted to the SEC that a fictitious October board meeting had been fabricated to approve those options
- The SEC ultimately did not sue Jobs, but did sue Apple's former CFO and former general counsel
- Jobs claimed he did not know the accounting details; that claim is still disputed

**Source**: [Stock option backdating scandal - Cult of Mac](https://www.cultofmac.com/apple-history/steve-jobs-apple-stock-backdating-scandal)

**Supply-chain labor issues**:
- After the Foxconn worker suicides, Jobs facilitated an arrangement under which about 100,000 vocational-school students became Foxconn interns, some as young as 15
- Apple was slow to respond to poor working conditions and low wages at overseas manufacturers
- Jobs's attitude toward this was more pragmatic than morally driven

**Source**: [Revealed: How Steve Jobs Violated Human Resource Ethics - HubPages](https://discover.hubpages.com/business/The-Downside-of-Steve-Jobs)

**Absence of philanthropy**:
- After returning to Apple, Jobs cancelled all corporate philanthropy programs
- He rarely made public donations (compared with Gates and other tech billionaires)
- His explanation: making great products is the greatest contribution to society

## IV. Academic and Business Analysis

### 4.1 Harvard Business Review's 14 leadership rules

Isaacson's article "The Real Leadership Lessons of Steve Jobs" in HBR (April 2012) distilled 14 rules:

1. **Focus**
2. **Simplify**
3. **Take responsibility end to end**
4. **When behind, leapfrog**
5. **Put products before profits**
6. **Don't be a slave to focus groups**
7. **Bend reality**
8. **Impute** (give products meaning)
9. **Push for perfection**
10. **Know both the big picture and the details**
11. **Tolerate only "A" players**
12. **Engage face-to-face**
13. **Combine the humanities with the sciences**
14. **Stay hungry, stay foolish**

**HBR's key insight**: Jobs's personality flaws and his leadership traits are two sides of the same coin; you cannot learn only his methodology while ignoring the personality traits that drove it.

**Source**: [The Real Leadership Lessons of Steve Jobs - HBR](https://hbr.org/2012/04/the-real-leadership-lessons-of-steve-jobs)

### 4.2 Harvard Business School case studies

HBS has published at least three formal Jobs-related cases:
- "Steve Jobs: Leader Strategist" (2015, revised 2018)
- "Steve Jobs: Changing the World" (2023)
- "Steve Jobs, the Immediate Case Study" (2011, released immediately after his death)

The common theme of these cases: Jobs proved that "product-driven leadership" is viable in large-scale enterprises, but its success depends heavily on the founder's personal judgment and authority.

**Source**: [Steve Jobs: Leader Strategist - HBS](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=49025)

## V. Observations from Biographers

### 5.1 Walter Isaacson, *Steve Jobs* (2011)

- Based on 40+ interviews with Jobs and interviews with 100+ people connected to him
- **Strengths**: enormous amount of information, covers the whole life
- **Criticism**: Isaacson tends to use Jobs's flaws to "add color" to the narrative but lacks deep analysis of his own; understands technical details poorly; uses "reality distortion field" repeatedly to the point of mechanical repetition
- **Dissatisfaction inside Apple**: Tim Cook said the book painted Jobs as "a greedy, selfish egomaniac"

### 5.2 Brent Schlender & Rick Tetzeli, *Becoming Steve Jobs* (2015)

- Focuses on Jobs's transformation between 1985 and 1997 (from being forced out of Apple to his return)
- **Core thesis**: At Pixar, Jobs learned not to interfere with talented subordinates and became a more patient leader with more strategic vision
- **Endorsement from inside Apple**: Tim Cook, Jony Ive, and Eddy Cue all publicly recommended it
- **Criticism**: "gradually abandons its critical distance and becomes a paean to the greatness of Jobs and Apple" — it reads more and more like a hymn
- **Key difference**: Isaacson wrote about a consistently genius tyrant; Schlender wrote about a person who went through growth and transformation

### 5.3 Adam Lashinsky, *Inside Apple* (2012)

- Focuses on Apple's organizational approach and management culture
- Argues that Jobs created not only products but a unique system of organizational operation
- Suggests that Jobs's complex personality cannot be simply summarized as "irresponsible"

### 5.4 Other books worth noting

- Andy Hertzfeld, *Revolution in the Valley* — records the birth of the Mac from a frontline engineer's perspective
- Lisa Brennan-Jobs, *Small Fry* — the daughter's perspective, covered in detail above
- Leander Kahney, *Jony Ive: The Genius Behind Apple's Greatest Products* — presents Jobs indirectly through Ive's story

## VI. Comparison with Contemporary Entrepreneurs

### 6.1 Jobs vs Gates: a clash of two technology philosophies

| Dimension | Steve Jobs | Bill Gates |
|------|-----------|------------|
| Core drive | Change the world, create the perfect product | Make software ubiquitous, business efficiency |
| Business model | Vertical integration (control the full stack) | Horizontal expansion (license to all hardware makers) |
| Design philosophy | Ultimate fusion of aesthetics + function | Function first, compatibility first |
| View of philanthropy | Making great products is the contribution | Systematic philanthropy (Gates Foundation) |
| Technical depth | An intuitive, not an engineer | A real programmer by background |
| Management style | Tyrannical, highly personal | More systematic, but equally demanding |
| Later-life relationship | Reconciled; the two talked for an afternoon before Jobs died | — |

**Core insight**: Gates won the battle for market share (Windows' dominance of the PC market), Jobs won the battle for brand and experience (Apple became the most valuable company in the world). The tension between the two philosophies still shapes the tech industry today (Android vs iOS is its continuation).

### 6.2 Jobs vs Bezos: product obsession vs customer obsession

| Dimension | Steve Jobs | Jeff Bezos |
|------|-----------|------------|
| Core drive | Product perfection | Customer satisfaction |
| Basis for decisions | "I know better than users what they want" | "Work backwards from customer needs" |
| Attitude toward focus groups | Contempt: "people don't know what they want" | Data-driven, but not blindly obedient |
| Pursuit of aesthetics | Extremely high | Pragmatism (the Amazon interface has never been pretty) |
| Diversification strategy | Focus on a few product lines | Constantly expanding into new fields |

### 6.3 Jobs vs Musk: two versions of the control freak

| Dimension | Steve Jobs | Elon Musk |
|------|-----------|-----------|
| Core drive | Create the perfect consumer electronics | Use technology to solve species-level problems |
| Technical involvement | Product decision-maker, not an engineer | Deeply involved in engineering details |
| Public image management | Extremely controlled (Apple's culture of secrecy) | Extremely open (livestreaming thoughts on Twitter) |
| Risk appetite | Disrupt within consumer electronics | Bet in life-and-death fields like aerospace and energy |
| Management style | Tyrant, but charming | Tyrant, but more direct and violent |
| Brand building | The product is the brand | The person is the brand |

**What they have in common**: all three have extremely high expectations, are hard to please, have the ability to create a "reality distortion," and believe their own judgment is superior to consensus.

**Jobs's uniqueness**: among this group, Jobs is the only one who put **aesthetics** and **the humanities** at the center. Musk cares about efficiency and the laws of physics, Bezos about customers and data, Gates about coverage and systems — only Jobs cared whether a product "feels" right.

## VII. Differences Between Assessments in His Lifetime and After His Death

### 7.1 Jobs's image in his lifetime

- Inside the tech industry: an awe-inspiring but unlikable genius; many avoided working with him
- Media: from the "loser" of the 1990s (ousted from Apple) to the "king of the comeback" of the 2000s (iPod/iPhone)
- Public: gradually rose from geek idol to cultural symbol
- Critics: a hypocritical hippie who talked Zen but acted like a tyrant

### 7.2 Mythologizing after his death

After Jobs died (October 5, 2011), a marked phenomenon of "mythologizing" occurred:

- **Religious mourning**: temporary shrines of candles, flowers, and handwritten letters appeared in front of Apple Stores. Scholars of religion analyzed this as "religious commemoration," with Jobs seen as Apple's "charismatic high priest"
- **Media sanctification**: TV anchors called him "our Edison, our Disney, our Da Vinci"
- **The "Apple is finished without Jobs" narrative**: widely spread by tech and business media, and later disproved by results in the Tim Cook era
- **Reinterpretation of personal flaws**: his cruelty to employees was repackaged as "the price of genius"; his control freakishness was repackaged as "insistence on quality"

### 7.3 The gap between myth and reality

| Mythical version | Real version |
|---------|---------|
| Jobs single-handedly invented the personal computer | The Apple II was designed by Wozniak; Jobs handled commercialization |
| Jobs was a born business genius | He made a great many management mistakes in Apple's early days and was expelled by his own board |
| Being ousted from Apple was unjust | His management style did cause serious internal problems at the company |
| Pixar was another of his glories | He originally wanted to sell hardware; it was John Lasseter who persuaded him toward animation |
| He was an unmitigated tyrant | At Pixar and in later Apple he learned more restraint and listening |
| Apple's success came from Jobs alone | The contributions of Ive, Cook, Schiller, Cue, and others were equally critical |
| He didn't care about family | In later life he deeply regretted neglecting Lisa and worked to be a better father |

### 7.4 Final regrets

In the last stage of his life Jobs told Isaacson: "I wanted my kids to know me. I wasn't always there for them, and I wanted them to know why and to understand what I did."

This statement itself breaks the pose he maintained all his life that "a perfectionist does not need to explain." Facing death, Jobs was more human than at any time during his life.

**Source**: [Steve Jobs experienced regret in final days - Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/steve-jobs-experienced-extreme-regret-174445552.html)

## VIII. Synthesis: Key Insights from Outside Views

### 8.1 The most consistent outside observations

Across colleagues, competitors, critics, and scholars, the following patterns are mentioned again and again:

1. **Extreme duality**: Jobs was not a "good person" or a "bad person" but both at extreme levels at once. In the same day he could make one employee cry and make another feel they were changing the world
2. **Taste as core competitive advantage**: even Gates admitted "I'd give a lot to have Steve's taste." Jobs's least replicable ability was aesthetic judgment
3. **Dispute over the transformation narrative**: Schlender holds that Jobs went through profound growth; Isaacson and critics hold that he essentially never changed and just learned to disguise it better
4. **Control as faith**: from product design to supply chain to retail to ecosystem, all of Jobs's decisions point to one word — control

### 8.2 The most controversial assessments

- **Was he a genius or a bully?** — Both, and inseparable
- **Was he an innovator or an integrator?** — He redefined what the word "innovation" means
- **Should his management style be learned?** — HBR's answer: learn his principles, but not his behavior
- **Is Apple better without Jobs?** — Cook proved the company can be bigger and more profitable, but no one thinks Apple in the Cook era has changed the world

### 8.3 The most underrated traits of Jobs in outside views

1. **Ability to learn**: learned managerial restraint from NeXT's and Pixar's failure/success (Schlender's core thesis)
2. **Narrative ability**: not only the speeches at product launches, but the ability to construct grand narratives for his own life and company
3. **Eye for people**: Ive, Cook, Forstall, Schiller — the team Jobs assembled kept running for 15 years after his death
4. **Contemplation of death**: the meditation on death in the Stanford speech was not a performance but part of his genuine philosophy of life
