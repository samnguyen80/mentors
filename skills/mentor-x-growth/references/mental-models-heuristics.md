## Core Mental Models (6)

### Model 1: The Lean Validation Flywheel

**In one line**: Post the smallest piece of content to validate first; expand if it works; let the expansion feed new content.

**How it works**:
```
Tweet (validate the idea)
  ↓ Data good?
Thread (expand in depth)
  ↓ Data good?
Newsletter/Blog (long-form asset)
  ↓ Data good?
Video/course/product (monetize)
  ↑ New ideas feed back ←──────┘
```

**Source**: Cole/Bush (Lean Writing), Sahil (225+ threads all validated first), Hormozi (tweet→video pipeline), Welsh (Content OS): four schools independently converging on the same pattern.

**Application**: Before writing long content, ask "Has this idea been validated with a single tweet?"

**Limitations**: The low-frequency, high-quality route (such as Karpathy) doesn't depend on this flywheel; it relies on personal authority and content scarcity. It suits the stage where you're still building an audience, not authorities who already have millions of followers.

### Model 2: Attention Engineering

**In one line**: The first 2 lines of every piece of content decide its fate, and the hook can be engineered.

**Core formula**:

Hook quality = curiosity gap × credibility × specificity

- **Curiosity gap** (Cole): reveal the beginning and the ending, hide the middle, forcing the reader to click
- **Credibility**: numbers, names, time anchors ("I studied 1000 accounts...")
- **Specificity**: add qualifiers until it's "uncomfortably specific" (Cole's Headline Checklist)

**Hormozi's Value Equation applied to Hooks**:
```
Hook value = (desired outcome × credibility) / (time cost × effort)
```
The bigger the numerator and the smaller the denominator, the more irresistible the hook.

**Algorithm validation**: X's Engagement Velocity mechanism: the engagement speed in the first 15-30 minutes decides whether a tweet enters a larger traffic pool or dies. The Hook determines performance in this window.

**Operating rules**:
- The Hook takes 50% of creation time (write 10-15 versions and pick the best, per Cole)
- Every headline must answer three questions: Who is it for? What is it about? Why read it?
- See `writing-workshop.md > Hook Improvement Examples` for before/after comparisons

**Limitations**: Over-optimizing the hook leads to clickbait. The content must deliver on the hook's promise, or long-term trust is drained.

### Model 3: Category Creation

**In one line**: Don't find a niche to squeeze into; create a category only you occupy.

**Three levels of evolution**:

| Level | Strategy | Example |
|-------|----------|---------|
| Beginner | Find a niche segment (niche down) | "AI tool reviews" |
| Intermediate | Interest Stack combination | "AI + indie development + product thinking" |
| Advanced | Category Creation | Invent new terms, redefine the niche |

**Cole's Snow Leopard theory**: Don't be a "lion" (competing in an existing category over who is strongest); be a "snow leopard" (occupying a unique position in a rare domain).

**Koe's Niche of One**:
- Don't find a niche; create one
- Formula: broad brand (share diverse interests) + narrow product (target a specific problem)
- Interest Stack: combine multiple interests to create a unique perspective (fitness + philosophy + business + lifestyle)

**Languaging (naming)**: Give your unique method its own name. Two words can change how a category is perceived (car → electric car). Ship 30 for 30 is itself a case of languaging.

**Tequila Test (category check)**:
1. List all the conventional advice on your topic
2. Cross it all out
3. Write what remains. If you can't write anything after crossing it out, you don't yet have a truly differentiated view

**Application**: when positioning is unclear, when you feel homogenized with others, when the niche feels too crowded.

**Limitations**: Category creation takes accumulated time and depth of expertise; in the cold-start phase you may need to build an audience inside an existing category first.

### Model 4: Value First

**In one line**: Give the secrets away free and sell the execution. Every piece of content is a value delivery.

**Hormozi's core insight**: Only 1% of people will do it themselves, while 99% are willing to pay for someone to do it for them. Give high-value content away free → prove you have the solution → build trust and reciprocity → convert naturally.

**Three-part content structure (Hook-Retain-Reward)**:
1. **Hook**: seize attention instantly (shock/question/bold promise)
2. **Retain**: keep delivering value (story + open loops + zero fluff)
3. **Reward**: over-deliver (actionable advice, more than promised)

**Welsh's education-first approach**: Build authority with educational content first, then steer toward monetization. The core of his 44K-follower gain in 18 weeks was "posting educational Tips in the afternoon".

**Sahil's Feynman-style validation**: If you can't explain a complex concept in the simplest words, you haven't understood it yet. Writing is the public practice of the Feynman technique.

**Application**: before writing each piece, ask "What can the reader do immediately after reading this?" If the answer is "nothing", rewrite.

**Limitations**: Pure value output doesn't build personal connection. Personal stories and opinions need to be interspersed (Dickie Bush's 75/25 rule: 75% breadth content pulls in new people, 25% depth content retains them).

### Model 5: Building in Public

**In one line**: Turn the process into content, making the audience stakeholders.

**Two variants**:

**Build in Public (levelsio)**:
- Publicly share revenue (MRR screenshots), process (feature iterations), failures (97% of projects fail)
- Core mechanism: the audience watches you go from 0 to $100K MRR and develops an "investor mindset"; they want you to succeed and spread the word on their own
- What to share: MRR milestones, feature launches, failure post-mortems, tech stack decisions, user feedback
- What not to share: exact customer acquisition cost, customers' personal information, core implementation details

**Learn in Public (swyx)**:
- Make the learning process public: write blogs, make tutorials, ask and answer in forums, creating "learning exhaust"
- **Pick Up What They Put Down**: when big names release something new, write a review/explainer/tutorial and tag them. They'll retweet, because "when someone praises my work, I can retweet it all day"
- You don't need to invent something new; you need to explain clearly what others invented

**Application**: the core differentiation strategy for the AI/tech niche. Suited to indie developers, creators building products, and technical learners.

**Limitations**: You must actually be building something. Pure commentary creators can't build in public. It also takes psychological resilience, since public means failures are public too.

### Model 6: Systematic Compounding

**In one line**: Replace inspiration with templates and systems, making content output a predictable machine.

**Welsh's Content OS**:
1. **Curate**: collect inspiration and well-performing content
2. **Templatize**: abstract successful content structures into templates
3. **Rapid Create**: use templates + material to produce 10-20 pieces of content per hour
4. **Distribute**: cross-platform + automation tools

**Koe's 2 Hour Writer**:
- 1 hour walking to find inspiration + 1 hour writing and editing
- Idea Museum: an organized material library
- Writing framework quick reference: Listicle / short post (personal redefinition / painful truth) / PSB story arc

**Content-reuse flywheel**:
```
Newsletter (1-2 long pieces a week)
  ├── Extract 5-7 short posts → Twitter/X
  ├── Screenshot Twitter posts → Instagram/LinkedIn
  ├── Read the newsletter aloud → YouTube
  └── High-engagement tweets → expand into next issue's newsletter topics
```

**Sahil's Notion board**: raw ideas → about to write → in progress → done but unpublished → published. Five columns of management so you never run out of content.

**Application**: when you feel you can't write, inspiration has dried up, or output is unstable. The system lets you produce decent content even on bad days.

**Limitations**: Over-systematizing makes content mechanical. Keep 20-30% of "non-system" space for inspiration and improvised reactions (especially hot-topic responses in the AI niche).

## Decision Heuristics (10)

### 1. Post the tweet before writing the long piece ← applies Model 1
Want to write long content? Validate with a single tweet first. "Twitter is an idea refinery, not a broadcast channel." (Bush)
- **Trigger**: any time you want to write a thread/newsletter/video
- **Action**: post a tweet testing the core point; expand if the data is good

### 2. The Hook takes 50% of creation time ← applies Model 2
Write 10-15 versions and pick the best. Every headline must answer: Who is it for? What is it about? Why read it? See "Hook Improvement Examples".
- **Trigger**: when starting to write any content

### 3. Conversation crushes everything
Algorithm weights: conversation reply 150x > Reply 27x > bookmark 20x > retweet 2x > Like 1x. One conversation that draws a reply from the author is worth more than 150 likes.
- **Trigger**: when thinking through an engagement strategy
- **Action**: write content that provokes replies (questions, controversial opinions, requests for feedback), and actively reply to every comment

### 4. The 1/3/1 rhythm
One hook sentence + three sentences of elaboration + one transition sentence. Makes content scannable. Single-sentence lines act like "checkpoints", giving the reader small dopamine hits.
- **Trigger**: writing any content longer than 3 sentences
- **Variants**: 1/4/1, 1/5/1, 1/2/5/2/1

### 5. The Super Bowl response (AI niche)
A new model release = the Super Bowl of the AI niche. Response timeline: 0-1h Quick Take → 1-6h Demo → 6-24h in-depth Thread → 1-7 days full review.
- **Trigger**: major releases of GPT/Claude/Gemini/open-source models
- **Key**: speed > perfection. The first insightful voice is worth more than the hundredth perfect analysis

### 6. Own your audience
Algorithms change; email lists don't. The ultimate goal of every tweet is to drive traffic to the newsletter. Twitter is the discovery engine (top of funnel), the newsletter is the deep relationship (owned audience).
- **Trigger**: when planning content strategy
- **Sahil's data**: newsletter ad revenue of $70K+ per month, all reinvested in growth

### 7. The 4A topic matrix
One topic × 4 angles = unlimited topics:
- **Actionable**: teach readers how to do it (Tips/Guides/How-to)
- **Analytical**: back it up with data (Stats/Trends/Frameworks)
- **Aspirational**: inspire possibility (Lessons/Mistakes/Habits)
- **Anthropological**: touch on human nature (Fears/Failures/Lies/Struggles)
- **Trigger**: when you feel "there's nothing to write about"

### 8. Give the secrets, sell the execution ← applies Model 4
When you hesitate over "should I share this free", the answer is give. 99% of people won't do it themselves. (Hormozi: zero ad spend, 1 million followers in 6 months)
- **Trigger**: when hesitating whether to share a method/tool/process for free

### 9. Templates beat inspiration
Abstract successful content into templates and use templates to produce quickly. Cole wrote 200+ threads using only 7 Thread templates. Welsh uses Content OS to produce 10-20 pieces of content an hour.
- **Trigger**: right after writing a piece of content that performs well, extract the template immediately
- **Cole's 7 templates**: Framework / Story / Actionable / Curation / Lessons / Mistakes / Contrarian

### 10. The comment section is a gold mine
Leaving high-quality replies in big accounts' comment sections = borrowing traffic. Welsh: one reply earned 6,700 impressions. Sahil in cold start: posted threads into the comment sections of 50 big accounts, and a single retweet from Chamath ignited growth.
- **Trigger**: the cold-start stage with <10K followers
- **Rule**: don't write a "good post"; write a 200-400 word "mini-newsletter-style reply"
