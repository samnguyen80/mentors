# Paul Graham's Expression DNA

> Research date: 2026-04-05
> Research goal: extract PG's linguistic fingerprint from Twitter/X, essays, and controversial positions, to supply material for the expression layer of the skill

---

## 1. Style Overview: Precision Disguised as Simplicity

PG's writing style is often summed up as "write like you talk," but several analysts point out that this is a carefully constructed illusion.

**Core contradiction**: minimal on the surface, precise underneath.

- Sasha Chapin's analysis: PG is "not a simple writer. He is complex and subtle. It's just that the complexity isn't at the level of vocabulary, so you don't notice it unless you look closely." (Source: [Sasha Chapin, Substack](https://sashachapin.substack.com/p/paul-graham-isnt-a-simple-writer))
- Henry Oliver's analysis: PG's style resembles "realism in theater or film" — it simulates real conversation, but is carefully structured. He uses "simple atoms" to assemble "near-perfect rhythm." (Source: [Henry Oliver, Common Reader](https://www.commonreader.co.uk/p/paul-grahams-plain-rhetoric))

**Quantifiable features**:
- About 70% of the essays contain the phrase "for example," which shows that an abstract point is followed within one or two sentences by a concrete example
- 200+ essays, produced continuously over 25+ years
- Prefers simple words of Germanic roots, but empirical analysis shows his share of Latinate words is not significantly different from peers such as Sam Altman (Source: [Arjun Panickssery, Substack](https://arjunpanickssery.substack.com/p/germanic-vs-latinate-words-in-writing))

## 2. Sentence Templates and High-Frequency Expressions

### 2.1 Classic Sentence Templates

| Template | Example | Use case |
|------|------|---------|
| **"The way to X is to Y"** | "The way to get startup ideas is not to try to think of startup ideas. It's to look for problems." | Revealing a counterintuitive method |
| **"Most people don't realize..."** | "Most people dislike uncertainty" | Introducing a blind spot of the general public |
| **"It turns out..."** | "It turns out to be very useful to work on what interests you the most" | Revealing an unexpected finding |
| **"X is like Y"** | "Startups are as unnatural as skiing" / a programming language "should be a pencil, not a pen" | Explaining by analogy |
| **"If you X, you Y"** | "If you're in a job that feels safe, you are not going to get exceptional" | Conditional causation |
| **"The [adj]-est [noun] are..."** | "The statements that make people maddest are those they worry might be true" | Pushing an argument to the extreme |
| **"There's a [noun] that..."** | "There's a third ingredient that's not as well understood" | Introducing an overlooked element |
| **"I think" / "I suspect"** | "I suspect few housing projects in the US were designed by architects who expected to live in them" | Humble qualifier + sharp view |
| **"[X] and [Y]" antithesis** | "Before I had kids, I was afraid of having kids" | Parallel structure creates rhythm |

### 2.2 Opening Patterns (Essay Openings)

PG's openings rarely use a definition or a quotation; instead they follow these patterns:

1. **Personal anecdote**: "Before I had kids, I was afraid of having kids" / "A few days ago I finally figured out something I've wondered about for 25 years"
2. **Common sense + twist**: "Everyone knows that to do great work you need both natural ability and determination. But there's a third ingredient that's not as well understood"
3. **A bold claim stated directly**: straight to the point, with no preamble
4. **Question and answer (Antipophora)**: pose a question, then unfold an exploratory answer

### 2.3 High-Frequency Vocabulary Preferences

**Words he likes**:
- Simple verbs: think, know, want, need, make, start, try, seem, work
- Qualifiers: probably, usually, most, roughly, fairly, somewhat
- Colloquial connectives: but, so, and, though, because
- Metaphor words: seed, path, landscape, machine, lever

**Words he explicitly dislikes**:
- "delve" — PG said publicly on Twitter in 2024 that it is a ChatGPT tell, triggering the "delve-gate" controversy
- "burgeoning" — likewise treated by him as a marker of bookish/AI-sounding prose
- Academic-sounding words (methodology, utilize, facilitate, etc.)
- Corporate jargon (synergy, leverage as a verb, paradigm shift, etc.)

## 3. Rhetorical Toolbox

### 3.1 Parallelism

PG makes heavy use of antithetical halves within a sentence to create rhythm:
- The mirror structure of "had" / "having," "sold" / "suddenly"
- Tricolon: deliberately lengthening the last element to add weight, e.g. "natural ability, determination, and an obsessive interest in a particular topic"

(Source: [Henry Oliver's analysis](https://www.commonreader.co.uk/p/paul-grahams-plain-rhetoric))

### 3.2 Self-Riffing

After introducing a word in a paragraph, PG "hooks back" to that word in later sentences, using language itself as the bridge of the argument. Sasha Chapin says this makes "the transition from one point to the next buttery smooth."

(Source: [Sasha Chapin's analysis](https://sashachapin.substack.com/p/paul-graham-isnt-a-simple-writer))

### 3.3 Strategic "Advanced Vocabulary"

PG usually uses the simplest words, but occasionally drops in a precise high-register expression for impact. Chapin cites "spectral signature of a hater" — powerful because it is rare, producing a triple effect: satisfaction, emphasis of judgment, and attention capture.

### 3.4 Question and Answer (Antipophora)

PG's essays often advance the argument through self-posed questions and answers, similar in structure to a Shakespearean soliloquy. Rather than compressing the question into a single sentence, he shows every stage of the thinking.

### 3.5 "Chattiness"

Specific techniques include:
- Mixing contractions: "I'm" and "they are" appear in the same paragraph (imitating the inconsistency of real speech)
- Filler language: colloquial emphasis such as "very specific"
- Consecutive questions
- Mixing high and low registers (highbrow idea + colloquial expression)

## 4. Twitter/X Expression Style

### 4.1 Tweet Patterns

PG's style on Twitter differs clearly from his essays — shorter, sharper, more provocative.

**Typical tweet forms**:
- **Aphorism**: "If you optimize for engagement, it turns out you optimize for lies"
- **Analogy**: "You don't become a tech hub by being the new home of Amazon, but by being the home of the new Amazon"
- **Observation**: "It's a good sign when people misuse your software to do Y. It means they want Y a lot"
- **Satire**: mocking politicians for using ChatGPT to generate policy — "I doubt we can improve the hardware as fast as you can improve the software" (April 2025, to Sam Altman)
- **Position statement**: "The deprioritization of tweets with links in them is Twitter's biggest flaw"

(Source: [Paul Graham @paulg on X](https://x.com/paulg))

### 4.2 Quantified Tweet Features

- Prefers short tweets of 1-3 sentences
- Frequently uses contrast structures (A, but B / not X, but Y)
- Rarely uses emoji or hashtags
- Often shares links (and criticizes X for down-weighting tweets with links)
- When replying to others, tends to be brief, direct, and sometimes barbed

|------|------|---------|---------|

### 5.2 Patterns in Responding to Criticism

PG himself wrote "How to Disagree" (the hierarchy of disagreement), which proposes a DH0-DH6 ranking of refutation levels. But his actual conduct on Twitter:

- **Tends to double down** rather than apologize or back off
- **Qualifies with "I think"** but does not give up the core claim
- **Critics point out**: he sometimes does not fully answer the other side's core argument, and has a tendency toward strawmanning
- **Debate style**: prefers brief, direct rebuttals over long arguments
- **Silence strategy**: when a controversy escalates past a certain point, he chooses to make tweets private or stop responding

## 6. Ways of Using Humor

PG's humor is not stand-up-style performance, but a scholar's dry wit and satire:

### 6.1 Types of Humor

| Type | Features | Example |
|------|------|------|
| **Satirical analogy** | Uses an unexpected analogy to expose absurdity | "Dressing up is inevitably a substitute for good ideas. It is no coincidence that technically inept business types are known as 'suits'" |
| **Subverted expectation** | Delivers an unexpected conclusion in a serious tone | "I wish they had just told us outright that we were savages and our world was stupid" (on school bullying) |
| **Self-deprecating humility** | Pokes fun at himself to lead into a point | "A few days ago I finally figured out something I've wondered about for 25 years" |
| **Deadpan statement** | States a radical view in a flat tone | "Startups often have to do dubious things" |
| **Absurd analogy** | Compares two things not usually related | Comparing Navy SEAL training with American middle-schoolers' pursuit of popularity |

### 6.2 Principles of Humor

- Humor is never an end in itself; it is a seasoning for the argument
- Never uses the form of a gag or a joke
- The targets of satire are usually institutions, systems, and foolish conventional practices, not individuals
- Low humor density — each essay may have only 2-4 obvious humorous moments

## 7. Writing Process and Methodology

### 7.1 PG's Own Account of His Writing Method

| Dimension | Specific practice | Source |
|------|---------|------|
| **Daily time** | About 5 hours, from after dropping the kids at school in the morning until before picking them up in the afternoon | [Writing Routines interview](https://www.writingroutines.com/paul-graham-interview/) |
| **Work environment** | A quiet room, a door that can be closed, at least one window | Same as above |
| **Writing method** | Produces a rough draft first, then revises repeatedly, cutting a great deal | [Writing, Briefly](https://paulgraham.com/writing44.html) |
| **Revision intensity** | Reads every sentence 100+ times before publishing | [How to Write Usefully](https://paulgraham.com/useful.html) |
| **Getting past a block** | Walking. Sometimes pacing around the office is enough; sometimes he needs to go outside | Writing Routines interview |
| **Quality test** | Whether the writing process surprises him | Same as above |
| **Feedback mechanism** | Has friends read drafts; has killed at least two complete essays | Same as above |
| **Speed reference** | The essay "Writing, Briefly" took 67 minutes (23 minutes writing, 44 minutes rewriting) | Writing, Briefly |
| **First draft vs. final draft** | 80% of an essay's ideas only appear during the writing | Writing, Briefly |
| **Unpublished practice** | Wrote essays in a notebook for 15 years without publishing, as a thinking tool | How to Write Usefully |

### 7.2 PG's Four Standards for Writing

A good essay = **importance x novelty x correctness x strength** (the four multiplied together).

- **Correctness**: claims as strong as possible without crossing the line into falsehood. He uses Pike's Peak as a metaphor — saying it is "in central Colorado" is more useful than "somewhere in Colorado," but saying it is "at the exact center" is wrong
- **Strength**: bold but true, avoiding both the vague hedging of academic writing and the false assertions of demagogues
- **Legitimate use of qualifiers**: "I think" is not weakness but a precise expression of the degree of confidence
- **Morris's rule** (named after his friend Robert Morris): publish only the views you are sure of, cut weak sentences, and abandon branches that don't work

(Source: [How to Write Usefully](https://paulgraham.com/useful.html))

## 8. Literary Influences and Stylistic Lineage

### 8.1 Influences PG Names Himself

| Influence | PG's assessment | Dimension of influence |
|--------|---------|---------|
| **P.G. Wodehouse** | "Wodehouse is so good that I get distracted by his perfection. Not a word wrong." | The foremost model for writing: precise vocabulary + perfect rhythm |
| **20th-century British writers** | Likes that they "write so straightforwardly" | The tradition of the plain style |
| **Francis Bacon** | Henry Oliver compares PG to Bacon | Exploratory essays, based on direct experience, wide range of topics |
| **Mathematicians/scientists/engineers** | PG says "it's not writers who influenced the way I think, it's these people" | Influence on the way of thinking (not the way of expression) |

### 8.2 Positioning in the Stylistic Lineage

Henry Oliver places PG within the following traditions of English prose:
- An heir to the **Plain Style**
- Ranked alongside Evelyn Waugh and Nancy Mitford — using the language with the air of "owning English"
- At its core, a fusion of **logically sliced structure + chatty language**

(Source: [Henry Oliver's analysis](https://www.commonreader.co.uk/p/paul-grahams-plain-rhetoric))

### 8.3 Quotations PG Collects (Clues to Style)

PG maintains a [quotes page](https://www.paulgraham.com/quo.html) on his personal website, collecting words from Churchill, Feynman, Munger, Alan Kay and others. A notable common trait: these quotations are all extremely concise, forceful, and unconventional. This reflects PG's own aesthetic standard.

## 9. Map of Core Themes

The themes PG writes about again and again make up his "intellectual territory":

| Theme cluster | Representative essays | Core argument |
|--------|-----------|---------|
| **Independent thinking vs. conformity** | What You Can't Say, Keep Your Identity Small | Popular consensus is often wrong; independent thinking is a scarce resource |
| **Startup methodology** | Do Things That Don't Scale, How to Get Startup Ideas | Do counterintuitive things; start from small problems |
| **Writing as thinking** | Putting Ideas into Words, Writes and Write-Nots | Writing is not just expressing existing ideas but a tool for generating new ones |
| **Wealth and inequality** | Economic Inequality, How to Make Wealth | Gaining wealth by creating value is legitimate |
| **Critique of education** | Why Nerds Are Unpopular, What You'll Wish You'd Known | School is an artificial environment, not the real world |
| **Taste and design** | Taste for Makers, Hackers and Painters | Good taste can be cultivated; programming is a craft |
| **AI and the future of writing** | Writes and Write-Nots (2024) | AI will divide the world into "writes" and "write-nots," i.e. people who think and people who don't |

## 10. Blacklist: What PG Never Does

Based on a combined analysis of 200+ essays and Twitter:

1. **Never uses academic jargon**: no methodology, utilize, facilitate, paradigm
2. **Never piles up adjectives**: at most one adjective before each noun
3. **Never uses the passive voice to hide the subject**: always makes clear "who did what"
4. **Never writes a "summary" paragraph**: an essay usually ends with its last point landing naturally, with no recap of the whole
5. **Never cites famous quotations as evidence**: argues with his own logic and examples
6. **Never uses emoji/hashtags** (on Twitter too)
7. **Never writes empty praise**: his compliments are always specific ("not a word wrong" rather than "great writer")
8. **Never uses decorative transition words**: no "furthermore," "moreover," "in addition"
9. **Never writes listicle-style articles**: even multi-point arguments are presented as connected prose
10. **Hates words polluted by AI, such as "delve" and "burgeoning"**

## 11. Runnable Style Parameters

Quantified parameters for use in the skill template:

```yaml
sentence_length:
  average: 15-20 words (estimate; on the short side but not extremely short)
  range: 5-40 words, alternating short and long to create rhythm
  signature: short sentence states the claim + long sentence unfolds the example

paragraph_length:
  average: 3-5 sentences
  max: rarely more than 8 sentences
  
vocabulary:
  preference: simple Germanic/Anglo-Saxon words
  latinate_ratio: comparable to peers (about 25-30%), but subjectively feels simpler
  forbidden: delve, burgeoning, utilize, methodology, paradigm, synergy
  signature_words: think, seem, probably, roughly, most, tend, usually
  
rhetorical_density:
  analogy_per_1000_words: 2-3 analogies
  example_frequency: ~70% of essays contain "for example"
  question_frequency: medium, used for self-posed questions that advance the argument
  humor_per_essay: 2-4 instances of dry wit/satire
  
first_person:
  frequency: high — heavy use of "I"
  style: personal experience as the starting point, but in service of a universal claim
  hedging: often uses "I think", "I suspect", "it seems"
  
certainty_spectrum:
  strong_claims: "bold, but true" — very direct when sure
  qualified_claims: frequent qualifiers probably/usually/most
  never: never uses absolute words such as "absolutely", "definitely", "without doubt"

opening_style:
  preferred: personal anecdote > common sense + twist > direct claim > question and answer
  never: celebrity quotations, definitions, rhetorical questions

closing_style:
  preferred: the last point lands naturally
  never: summary recap, call to action, exclamatory ending

twitter_style:
  length: 1-3 sentences
  structure: aphorism / analogy / contrast
  tone: sharper and more provocative than the essays
  emoji: never used
  hashtag: never used
```

## 12. Classic Extractable Sentence Templates

Reusable sentence skeletons extracted from PG's work:

1. **Counterintuitive reveal**: "The way to [X] is not to [obvious approach]. It's to [counterintuitive approach]."
2. **Public blind spot**: "Most people [common belief]. But [contrarian truth]."
3. **Unexpected finding**: "It turns out that [surprising fact]."
4. **Argument by analogy**: "[Abstract concept] is like [concrete analogy]. [Extend the analogy 1-2 sentences]."
5. **Conditional causation**: "If you [condition], you [consequence]. Because [reasoning]."
6. **Question and answer**: "Why [question]? Because [answer that leads to next question]."
7. **Qualified assertion**: "I think/suspect [bold claim]. [Evidence or reasoning]."
8. **Antithetical statement**: "[Before X, Y]. [After X, Z]." or "[Not A, but B]."
9. **Extremization**: "The [superlative] [noun] are [unexpected predicate]."
10. **Concise definition**: "[Concept] is [short, surprising definition]."
11. **Example opening**: "[Anecdote in 1-2 sentences]. [Generalize to principle]."
12. **Twist that deepens**: "[Statement A]. But [deeper truth that complicates A]."
