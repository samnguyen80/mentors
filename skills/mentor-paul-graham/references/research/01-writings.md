## 1. Background

**Paul Graham** (born November 13, 1964) is an English-American computer scientist, writer, essayist, entrepreneur, and investor.

### Key career milestones
- **Birth**: Weymouth, Dorset, England; moved with his family to Pittsburgh, USA at age 4
- **Education**: BA from Cornell University (1986) → Master's and PhD in CS from Harvard University → studied painting at RISD (Rhode Island School of Design) and the Accademia di Belle Arti in Florence
- **Viaweb** (1995-1998): Co-founded with Robert Morris; online store-building software written in Common Lisp. Acquired by Yahoo in 1998 for $49.6 million and became Yahoo Store
- **Y Combinator** (2005 onward): Co-founded with Trevor Blackwell, Jessica Livingston, and Robert Morris. Has invested in 1,300+ startups (Reddit, Dropbox, Airbnb, Stripe, etc.)
- **The Arc language**: Announced in 2001 as a new Lisp dialect; released in 2008
- **Books**: *On Lisp*, *ANSI Common Lisp*, *Hackers & Painters*
- **Essays**: Published 200+ essays on paulgraham.com from 1998 to 2026, roughly one every 4-8 weeks

> Sources: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Graham_(programmer)), [paulgraham.com/bio.html](https://paulgraham.com/bio.html) | Primary + credible

## 2. Core Book: *Hackers & Painters*

**Published**: 2004, O'Reilly Media
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/hp.html

### Core arguments

1. **Programming is making, not science**: Hackers (programmers) and painters are both makers. Graham thinks the term "computer science" is problematic: it is not really a science, but a pile of fields thrown together by historical accident
2. **Learn by doing**: Painting is learned mainly by doing, and so is programming. Most hackers did not learn programming in university courses; they taught themselves by writing programs at age 13
3. **A programming language should be a pencil, not a pen**: A language should be malleable, used to think about programs, not just to express programs you have already thought of
4. **Iterative development**: Painting starts with a sketch and is refined step by step, and programming should work the same way. Sometimes the original plan turns out to be wrong
5. **Empathy is a core ability**: Hackers, like painters, must have empathy and see things from the user's point of view in order to do great work

> Sources: [paulgraham.com/hp.html](https://paulgraham.com/hp.html), [Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/41793.Hackers_Painters), [Medium analysis](https://medium.com/@edisipka/my-notes-on-paul-grahams-hackers-and-painters-why-programming-is-actually-art-9a4829117554) | Primary + secondary

## 3. Writing Methodology: Essays (6 core pieces)

### 3.1 The Age of the Essay (2004)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/essay.html

- An essay should not be "proving a thesis" but **exploration**
- The way writing is taught in school (the five-paragraph argument) distorts the nature of the essay
- The word "essay" comes from the French "essai," meaning "to try"; Montaigne invented the genre
- A good essay is the process of thinking, not the result of thinking

> Source: primary | Credibility: ★★★★★

### 3.2 Writing, Briefly (2005)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/writing44.html

Graham's codified writing rules:
- Write a bad version 1 as fast as you can, then rewrite it over and over
- If you get stuck, tell someone what you're going to write about, then write down what you said
- Expect that 80% of the ideas in an essay will appear only after you start writing
- Start with the first sentence
- Read the essay aloud to find awkward phrases and boring passages

> Source: primary | Credibility: ★★★★★

### 3.3 Write Like You Talk (2015)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/talk.html

- There should be an interconnection between writing and speaking
- Advice: when you read your essay, read it aloud and change everything that doesn't sound like conversation
- Following this method already puts you "ahead of 95% of writers"

> Source: primary | Credibility: ★★★★★

### 3.4 Write Simply (2021)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/simply.html

- "I try to write using ordinary words and simple sentences. That kind of writing is easier to read, and the easier something is to read, the more deeply readers will engage with it."
- Simple language, simple vocabulary, simple sentences, but without lowering the depth of the ideas
- Writing simply is a *choice*, not a lack of ability

> Source: primary | Credibility: ★★★★★

### 3.5 How to Write Usefully (2020)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/useful.html

- Formula for useful writing = **importance × novelty × correctness × strength**
- Of the four variables, novelty is the easiest to overlook
- Writing is not just conveying information; it should change how people think about something

> Source: primary | Credibility: ★★★★★

### 3.6 Putting Ideas into Words (2022)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/words.html

- **Core thesis**: Writing is thinking
- The process of putting ideas into words forces you to think more clearly
- Many people think they have worked things out before writing, but they haven't; the writing process itself produces new understanding
- This thesis echoes the 2024 essay "Writes and Write-Nots"

> Source: primary | Credibility: ★★★★★

### The unified core of the writing methodology

Graham's philosophy of writing boils down to:
1. **Writing = thinking** (recurs ≥5 times)
2. **Simple > complex** (recurs ≥4 times)
3. **Spoken > written style** (recurs ≥3 times)
4. **Iterative writing**: write a bad draft first → revise repeatedly (recurs ≥3 times)
5. **Exploration > argument** (recurs ≥3 times)

## 4. Startup Essays (core titles)

### 4.1 How to Start a Startup (2005)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/start.html

Three things for startup success:
1. Start with good people
2. Make something customers actually want
3. Spend as little money as possible

Other key arguments:
- Cofounders are to a startup what location is to real estate
- Launch fast: "you haven't really started working until you've launched"
- Making a few people really happy > making a lot of people somewhat happy

> Source: primary | Credibility: ★★★★★

### 4.2 Do Things that Don't Scale (2013)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/ds.html

- **Core thesis**: Early-stage founders should embrace manual, labor-intensive effort even when it doesn't scale
- The most common unscalable thing: recruiting users manually
- "If you can find someone with a problem that needs solving and you can solve it manually, go ahead and do that for now. This is much better than having something automated that nobody needs."
- 10 customers + 10% weekly growth → exponential growth takes care of the base-number problem

> Source: primary | Credibility: ★★★★★

### 4.3 Startup = Growth (2012)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/growth.html

- **A startup is defined not by being young or small, but by growth**
- A good growth rate during YC is 5-7% a week; 10% is excellent
- $1,000/month + 1% weekly growth → $7,900/month after 4 years
- $1,000/month + 5% weekly growth → $25 million/month after 4 years
- The compounding effect of small percentages produces completely different outcomes

> Source: primary | Credibility: ★★★★★

### 4.4 Default Alive or Default Dead? (2015)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/aord.html

- Founders should know whether their company is "default alive" or "default dead"
- The calculation needs four inputs: current expenses, current revenue, current growth rate, and cash on hand
- Default-alive companies have more negotiating leverage
- **Hiring too fast is the number one killer of startups after funding**
- The "fatal pinch": default dead + slow growth + no time to fix it

> Source: primary | Credibility: ★★★★★

### 4.5 Frighteningly Ambitious Startup Ideas (2012)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/ambitious.html

- The most ambitious startup ideas are frightening because they are really hard
- Lists several "frighteningly ambitious" directions

> Source: primary | Credibility: ★★★★★

### 4.6 Schlep Blindness (2012)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/schlep.html

- "Schlep blindness": people fail to see great startup ideas because those ideas involve schleps, a Yiddish word for tedious, unpleasant tasks
- Many of the best startup opportunities are overlooked because people instinctively avoid "dirty work"
- The Collison brothers at Stripe are a counterexample to schlep blindness: they were willing to tackle payments, an area nobody wanted to touch

> Sources: primary + [LinkedIn discussion](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/the-startup-archive_alexandr-wang-on-why-paul-grahams-schlep-activity-7369036483380314112-nd2v) | Credibility: ★★★★★

### 4.7 How to Get Startup Ideas (2012)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html

- The best startup ideas have three things in common: the founders want it themselves, they can build it, and few others realize it's worth doing
- "The way to succeed is not to become an expert on startups, but to become an expert on your users and the problem"

> Source: primary | Credibility: ★★★★★

### 4.8 Founder Mode (September 2024)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/foundermode.html

- Inspired by a talk by Airbnb co-founder Brian Chesky at a YC event
- **Two modes of running a company**: Founder Mode vs Manager Mode (professional-manager mode)
- Silicon Valley's conventional wisdom is that once a company gets big you should switch to manager mode; Graham thinks this is wrong
- Chesky found that the "hire good people and give them room to do their jobs" approach was disastrous for Airbnb
- Chesky studied how Jobs ran Apple, shifted to founder mode, and things improved dramatically
- Founders should go deep into the product details, like a CPO
- **This was PG's most viral essay of 2024** and set off discussion across the whole tech world

> Sources: primary + [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2024/09/01/paul-graham-founder-mode-silicon-valley-conventional-wisdom-manager-mode/), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Founder_mode) | Credibility: ★★★★★

## 5. Philosophy of Life and Cognition Essays

### 5.1 How to Do Great Work (2023)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/greatwork.html

Four-step framework:
1. Choose a field you have interest in and aptitude for
2. Learn enough to get to the frontier of knowledge
3. Notice gaps, patterns, and anomalies that others overlook
4. Explore the most promising gaps

Key arguments:
- **Curiosity is the key to doing great work**: it helps you choose a field, reach the frontier, notice the gaps, and drives the exploration
- The factors (in the mathematical sense) of great work: ability, interest, effort, luck
- "Writing a page a day doesn't sound like much, but if you do it every day it adds up to a book a year": the cumulative effect of consistency
- Doing great work = doing important things so well that you expand people's sense of what's possible
- Follow genuine interest rather than prestige

> Source: primary | Credibility: ★★★★★

### 5.2 Superlinear Returns (2023)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/superlinear.html

- **Superlinear returns**: double the input and the output may quadruple or more
- Two drivers: **exponential growth (compounding)** and **thresholds (winner take all)**
- Science has the highest superlinear returns because it combines learning, thresholds, and new discoveries
- The most obvious way to exploit superlinear returns: do extraordinarily good work; far out on the curve, marginal effort is a bargain and there is less competition
- **Always be learning**: if you're not learning, you're probably not on a path to superlinear returns

> Source: primary | Credibility: ★★★★★

### 5.3 Life is Short (2016)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/vb.html

Three core guidelines for action:
1. **Ruthlessly prune bullshit**: unnecessary meetings, pointless arguments, bureaucracy, pretension
2. **Don't wait**: don't wait to climb that mountain, write that book, or visit your mother
3. **Savor the time you have**

Personal touchpoint:
- "After my mother died, I wished I'd spent more time with her. I lived as if she'd always be there."
- Two ways bullshit enters your life: you're forced to accept it, or you're tricked into accepting it

> Source: primary | Credibility: ★★★★★

### 5.4 The Bus Ticket Theory of Genius (2019)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/genius.html

- The recipe for genius = **a disinterested obsession with something that matters**
- "Disinterested" is the most important feature: not to impress others or get rich, but for the thing itself
- The path to new ideas often doesn't look promising; if it did, others would have explored it already
- Tests: you're making rather than consuming, the thing you're interested in is hard, and that difficulty is easier for you than for others

> Source: primary | Credibility: ★★★★★

### 5.5 Keep Your Identity Small (2009)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/identity.html

- "The more labels you have for yourself, the dumber they make you."
- Once a topic becomes part of your identity, you can no longer think about it rationally
- Religion and politics provoke the fiercest arguments not because they are special in themselves, but because people fold them into their identity

> Source: primary | Credibility: ★★★★★

### 5.6 What You Can't Say (2004)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/say.html

- Every age has beliefs that people take to be true but are in fact absurd; ours is unlikely to be the first age in which everyone got everything right
- **The test**: Do you have any opinions that you would be reluctant to express in front of your peers? If not, it is unlikely to be a coincidence; more likely you are just thinking what you've been told
- Ways to identify hidden taboos: look at what gets people in trouble for saying it, identify the labels used to silence people, and compare across cultures and eras

> Source: primary | Credibility: ★★★★★

### 5.7 How to Think for Yourself (2020)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/think.html

Three components of independent-mindedness:
1. Fastidiousness about truth
2. Resistance to being told what to think
3. Curiosity

"If your answers all turn out to be the ones you were supposed to believe, it's unlikely to be a coincidence"

> Source: primary | Credibility: ★★★★★

### 5.8 The Four Quadrants of Conformism (2020)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/conformism.html

Four types of people:
1. **Aggressively conventional-minded**
2. **Passively conventional-minded**
3. **Passively independent-minded**
4. **Aggressively independent-minded**

> Source: primary | Credibility: ★★★★★

### 5.9 What You'll Wish You'd Known (2005)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/hs.html

- An unpublished graduation speech for high school students
- Don't panic about having a "life goal"; most successful people find it along the way
- **"Stay upwind"**: like a glider, at each stage do the most interesting thing that gives you the most options for the future
- Curiosity never lies; it knows better than you do what is worth paying attention to

> Source: primary | Credibility: ★★★★★

### 5.10 Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule (2009)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/makersschedule.html

- **Two kinds of schedule**: the manager's schedule (cut into hours) vs the maker's schedule (units of at least half a day)
- For makers, a single meeting can wreck an entire afternoon, because it cuts the time into two pieces, each too small to do anything hard
- Power usually sits with managers, who make everyone resonate at their own frequency
- Graham's solution: concentrate all meetings at the end of the workday (office hours)

> Source: primary | Credibility: ★★★★★

### 5.11 Mean People Fail (2014)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/mean.html

- Among the most successful people Graham knows, almost none are mean
- Meanness makes you stupid: you can't do your best work in a fight
- Mean founders can't attract the best people
- Doing great things requires a spirit of benevolence
- Most success in history was zero-sum, where meanness may have been an advantage, but startups are not

**Controversy/contradiction**: Critics point out that successful founders such as Jobs, Zuckerberg, and Bezos all had a mean side. Graham's argument may be too idealistic.

> Sources: primary + [Inc. rebuttal](https://www.inc.com/jeff-bercovici/paul-graham-mean-people-fail.html) | Credibility: ★★★★☆ (disputed)

### 5.12 The Submarine (2005)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/submarine.html

- PR firms are "like a huge, silent submarine lurking beneath the news"
- In news other than politics/crime/disasters, more than half may come from PR
- PR firms feed the same story to multiple publications at once; readers think it's a trend, when it is actually manufactured
- The weakness of top journalists is vanity rather than laziness

> Source: primary | Credibility: ★★★★★

## 6. Technical/Programming Essays

### 6.1 Beating the Averages (2003)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/avg.html

- **The Blub paradox**: Imagine a Blub programmer. When he looks down the power continuum, he thinks lower-level languages lack features. When he looks up, he can't tell he's looking up; he only sees "weird languages with lots of useless stuff added"
- "The only programmers in a position to see all the differences in power between the various languages are those who understand the most powerful one."
- Writing Viaweb's software in Lisp was a key competitive advantage: features shipped faster than competitors'

> Source: primary | Credibility: ★★★★★

### 7.1 Writes and Write-Nots (2024/2025)
**URL**: https://paulgraham.com/writes.html

- Predicts that the AI era will produce a split into "writes" and "write-nots"
- "Not good writers, mediocre writers, and non-writers: only good writers and non-writers"
- **Writing is thinking**: people who skip developing writing skills also skip learning to think clearly

- **"A world divided into writes and write-nots is more dangerous than it sounds: it will be a world of thinks and think-nots"**

## 11. Writing Style DNA Analysis

Based on secondary analyses of Graham's writing and a reading of the primary essays:

### Syntactic features
- Prefers **short sentences and short words**, yet expresses sophisticated ideas
- ~70% of essays include an "example": abstract ideas are usually followed within a sentence or two by a well-chosen example
- Heavy use of the second person "you," speaking directly to the reader
- Almost no jargon; uses the most ordinary words to express unordinary ideas

### Structural features
- No five-paragraph structure; instead **free-form, essay-style exploration**
- Usually starts from an observation or a question and unfolds step by step
- Often turns with "incidentally," "in fact," "it turns out"
- Endings are often open-ended, without a summarizing close

### Rhetorical devices
- **Analogy and metaphor** are the most commonly used tools ("programming is like painting," "a startup is like a glider," "ideas are like moral fashions")
- Rhetorical questions ("If all your beliefs are ones you're supposed to believe, could that be a coincidence?")
- Lists (often places a key list in the middle of an essay)
- Self-correction ("I may be wrong, but...", "There may be exceptions...")

### Thinking features
- **From the specific to the general**: first tell a concrete story/case, then distill a general principle
- **Inverted thinking**: often first asks "what is wrong?" and then derives "what is right?"
- **Cross-domain analogy**: frequent jumps among painting → programming → startups → writing
- **Honesty about uncertainty**: admitting being unsure or possibly wrong recurs across the essays

> Sources: [Ellen Fishbein analysis](https://ellenrhymes.com/paul-graham), [Quora discussion](https://www.quora.com/What-makes-Paul-Grahams-essays-so-good), [Billy Oppenheimer](https://billyoppenheimer.com/paul-graham-essays/) | Secondary + partly primary

## 12. Record of Contradictions and Controversies

### Contradiction 1: Mean People Fail vs reality
- **PG's position**: mean people fail in startups
- **Counterexamples**: Jobs, Zuckerberg, Bezos, and others are widely regarded as having a mean side yet were extremely successful
- **Possible reconciliation**: PG may mean pure meanness (the incompetent kind), not being "demanding"

### Contradiction 2: Founder Mode vs earlier advice
- **PG 2024**: founders should be deeply involved in operational details
- **PG earlier**: several essays advised founders to focus on the most important things and to delegate
- **Possible explanation**: PG's thinking evolved, and Founder Mode is a correction to his earlier delegation advice

### Contradiction 3: The controversy over his views on economic inequality
- PG once wrote an essay defending economic inequality, arguing it is a byproduct of innovation
- It drew critical articles from outlets such as Quartz
- This position is in tension with some of his "benevolence"-related arguments

### Contradiction 4: AI optimism vs AI worry
- **Optimistic side**: AI is "the missing piece in a large number of puzzles," an important technology
- **Worried side**: AI will lead to a split into "writes and write-nots" / "thinks and think-nots"
- The two are not fully contradictory, but they reflect Graham's complex attitude toward AI

## 14. Research Summary

### Key findings

1. **Paul Graham is one of the most influential essay writers of our time**: his 200+ essays cover four major areas: startups, writing, programming, and philosophy of life

2. **His thinking is highly consistent and interconnected**: curiosity → independent thinking → writing is thinking → doing great work → superlinear returns, forming a complete intellectual system

3. **His writing style is the best proof of his methodology**: he expresses the deepest ideas in the simplest words, distilling general principles from firsthand experience (Viaweb, YC)

4. **The two most influential pieces of 2024**: Founder Mode (redefining company management) and Writes and Write-Nots (anticipating AI's effect on human thinking ability)

5. **He coined a series of terms that have entered everyday Silicon Valley vocabulary**: ramen profitable, schlep blindness, do things that don't scale, founder mode, Blub paradox, and more

6. **His core contradiction** is the tension between idealism (mean people fail, benevolence as a driver) and reality, though he usually admits he might be wrong
