## 1. Evaluations by Peers and Colleagues

### Sam Altman

- When Karpathy returned to OpenAI for the second time (February 2023), Altman tweeted "@karpathy welcome back!" on X. ★★★
- **Core disagreement**: Altman predicts AI will surpass human intelligence in any professional domain before 2030; Karpathy says his own AGI timeline is "5 to 10 times more pessimistic than the mainstream forecast". Karpathy has publicly rebutted the optimistic predictions of Altman, Dario Amodei, and Jensen Huang directly, saying they "overstate what AI can do". ★★★ (Fortune report, October 2025)

### Ilya Sutskever

- The two left OpenAI at almost the same time (Karpathy in February 2024, Sutskever in June 2024), but for entirely different reasons and with entirely different trajectories.
- Sutskever took part in the November 2023 board coup against Altman; Karpathy has maintained a friendly relationship with OpenAI throughout, and his path clearly diverges from Sutskever's.
- An outside observer: "The two represent two fundamentally different visions: AI will become your tutor (Karpathy) and AI will become your god (Sutskever)." ★★

### Andrew Ng

Both are central figures in deep learning education, but they clashed head-on over the concept of "vibe coding".

Ng said at the LangChain Interrupt event in June 2025: "It's unfortunate that this thing is called vibe coding; the name misleads people into thinking engineers just go on feel. In reality, guiding an AI to write useful software is deeply intellectual work. I spend whole days coding with AI assistance, and frankly, at the end I'm exhausted." ★★★

Ng is not rejecting AI-assisted programming itself, but arguing that Karpathy's naming creates a cognitive misdirection.

### Richard Sutton (founder of the RL field)

There is a substantive academic disagreement between Karpathy and Sutton. Karpathy put forward "we're summoning ghosts", rebutting Sutton's "we're building animals" framework.

Sutton thinks LLMs are a "dead end", stressing that RL and continual learning are the right path; Karpathy does not endorse RL as the main route, calling it "sucking supervision through a straw", with a fundamental noise problem. ★★★

### Fei-Fei Li (PhD advisor)

The two co-taught Stanford CS231n, which grew from 150 students in 2015 to 750 in 2017, indirectly confirming the high regard the outside world has for the course. No direct public statement by Fei-Fei Li evaluating Karpathy was found. ★★ (indirect evidence)

## 2. Industry Reactions to His Departures

### Leaving Tesla (July 2022)

The industry reaction was fairly shocked. Fortune's headline: "Who is Andrej Karpathy? Tesla's AI chief resigns abruptly, spelling trouble for Elon Musk." ★★★

An outside analysis (Medium): the "real reason" for leaving may have been a long-standing tension between Musk's overly optimistic public commitments on FSD and Karpathy's actual engineering understanding — Karpathy never publicly exaggerates progress. ★ (speculative analysis, limited credibility)

### Leaving OpenAI (February 2024)

Karpathy's own wording: "Nothing happened, it was not the result of any event or drama." TechCrunch's headline: "Andrej Karpathy leaves OpenAI again — but he says there was no drama." ★★★

In contrast with Sutskever's departure in the same period, outside media generally reported the two together, but the actual reasons were completely different: Karpathy's was a voluntary choice, Sutskever's an exit after a political defeat.

## 3. The Controversy Sparked by the "Vibe Coding" Concept

### Original definition

"There's a new kind of coding I call vibe coding — you fully give in to the vibes, embrace exponentials, and forget that the code even exists." ★★★

### Supporters' arguments

Simon Willison (co-founder of Django): praised Karpathy's original definition highly, finding it "precise and fun", because Karpathy is a top programmer, and what he describes with the term is a specific mode of exploration, not an argument for giving up understanding. ★★★

### Critics' arguments

1. **Andrew Ng's naming critique**: the term itself is misleading, making people think engineering is "going on feel", when AI-assisted programming is in fact heavy intellectual work. ★★★
2. **Security vulnerability risk**: a December 2025 CodeRabbit analysis found that AI-collaborated code has a 2.74x higher rate of security vulnerabilities than human code. ★★★
3. **Maintainability problems**: Fast Company reported on the "vibe coding hangover" — senior engineers described falling into "development hell" after inheriting AI-generated codebases. ★★★
4. **Skill erosion among beginners**: critics worry vibe coding eliminates the entry-level tasks novices need to learn programming, damaging the skills pipeline. ★★

### The 2026 reversal

Karpathy himself declared vibe coding "outdated"; his new preferred term is "agentic engineering": "By default, you're not writing code directly 99% of the time; you're orchestrating agents and acting as oversight." ★★★

## 4. The "Job Risk Map" Deletion Incident (March 2026)

### What happened

Karpathy "vibe coded" in two hours an interactive chart that scored 342 BLS occupations for AI exposure (0-10). The chart showed white-collar jobs scoring highest and manual-labor jobs lowest. Elon Musk reposted it with the comment "all jobs will be optional", and the chart went viral.

Within hours, Karpathy deleted the GitHub repository. His explanation: "'Exposure' was scored by an LLM based on how digital a job is. It has nothing to do with what will actually happen to those jobs. People are distorting this visualization and putting words in my mouth." ★★★

### Community criticism

- **Methodological flaw**: using LLM scoring as a proxy for the labor market is methodologically too crude.
- Some observers read the incident as the cost of Karpathy's "culture of experimenting in public": he is willing to share half-finished ideas publicly, but when media amplify them, he chooses to retreat rather than engage in the debate. ★★

## 5. External Evaluations of Eureka Labs

### Anticipation

TechCrunch's coverage was positive in tone, treating it as a natural extension: from Stanford CS231n to YouTube teaching videos to a formal startup. ★★★

### Doubts and criticism (Dan Meyer, a math educator)

Dan Meyer wrote "Andrej Karpathy Is in Trouble" on Substack, the most weighty public criticism so far:

- **Precedents of past failures**: Sebastian Thrun's Udacity and Andrew Ng's Coursera were both pioneers by tech elites in online education, but neither achieved the grand goal of educational transformation.
- **The core contradiction**: "Few of the people who design educational software have experience successfully running a classroom or a school." Karpathy helped build the world's most advanced computing technology, but he needs to pour all his creativity into the harder task of "helping people learn".
- **History of failed scaling of learning**: "Every technology that has promised to scale learning has failed to live up to its hype." ★★★

## 6. Evaluations from the AI Learner Community

### Highly positive evaluations (the mainstream voice)

- Google Scholar shows over 78,000 citations (at the time of research). ★★★
- The "Zero to Hero" course is widely regarded as one of the best introductory courses in deep learning. DeepLearning.AI listed him in "Heroes of Deep Learning". ★★★
- His teaching style is highly praised as "genuine": stressing "don't abstract anything away", coding live and showing the fixing of errors.

### Subtle criticisms (a minority voice)

- A few learners think the course assumes considerable background, so the name "zero to hero" is somewhat exaggerated.
- Discussion of Eureka Labs on Hacker News: some are looking forward to it, some take a "show me" wait-and-see stance.

## 8. Behavioral Patterns Observed from Outside

### 1. A culture of public experimentation, but sometimes a hasty wrap-up
The job risk map incident is a typical case: publish a half-finished item → go viral → delete and clarify. Do first, think later, but when the social consequences exceed expectations, he chooses to retreat rather than debate. ★★★

### 2. Willing to oppose industry consensus
During the AGI bubble, he was one of the few top figures willing to say publicly "models are not there" and "the products are slop". The TradeFox CEO: "If this Karpathy interview can't puncture the AI bubble, nothing can." ★★★ (Fortune, October 2025)

### 3. Speaks fast, with thought ahead of expression
Karpathy himself admits: "I know, I speak too fast. It works against me, because sometimes my speaking thread executes faster than my thinking." ★★★

### 4. An intriguing relationship with Elon Musk
Musk reposted his job risk map and the two seem to stay in touch, but Karpathy has never publicly endorsed Musk's political actions. His leaving Tesla has been analyzed as reflecting an underlying tension with Musk's culture of "overly optimistic public commitments". ★ (speculative, no direct evidence)

### 5. A low-profile personal life, high-profile technical views
No credible reporting on his private life was found. His public and private personas almost completely coincide — technical blog posts, course videos, technical commentary on X.

## 10. Core Differentiating Traits (External Observation)

Compared with AI leaders of the same generation, outside observers generally note the following distinctive points:

1. **Dual scarcity**: he is both a top engineer and a top communicator. LeCun can research, but his communication is highbrow and hard to follow; many popularizers can explain but lack engineering depth.
2. **Institutional independence**: he moves among Stanford, Tesla, OpenAI, and Eureka Labs without being attached to any single institution, which makes his public statements more credible.
3. **A constructive critic**: he criticizes AI hype but does not deny AI's value — in sharp contrast to the "anti-AI" stance of people like Gary Marcus.
4. **Conceptual productivity**: "Software 2.0" (2017), "vibe coding" (2025), "summoning ghosts" (2025), "agentic engineering" (2026) — he regularly contributes vocabulary that circulates within the industry.
5. **Public vulnerability**: he is willing to say publicly "I've never felt this much behind as a programmer" (2025), and to admit he speaks too fast, and so on — rare among top AI leaders.
