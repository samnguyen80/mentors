# 01 · The book and the 11 questions

Notes summarized in the skill author's own words; this is not a verbatim copy of the book.

## Sources
- https://tim.blog/2017/10/03/tribe-of-mentors/ (sample chapter: the introduction)
- https://tim.blog/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/Tools-of-Titans-and-Tribe-of-Mentors-Introductions-and-Profiles.pdf (an excerpt Tim Ferriss published himself: the introduction plus 4 profiles)

## How the book came about
- In 2017 Tim Ferriss turned 40, his first book, The 4-Hour Workweek, was 10 years old, a few friends had died, and he realized he had no plan for life after 40.
- He had a long list of big questions about goals, priorities, how to treat himself, and how to say "no" to noise so he could say "yes" to what he really wanted. The list overwhelmed him.
- He asked himself: "What would this look like if it were easy?" Answering that question by hand produced the idea of sending those same questions to a "tribe of mentors".
- Result: more than 100 people answered (roughly 130-140 profiles in the book), and at least half of his dream list did not reply or declined (for example, no Dalai Lama).

## How it was done
- The same set of 11 questions went to everyone; each respondent picked the 3-5 they liked best.
- 8 of the questions came from the "rapid-fire" segment of the podcast, honed over 300+ interviews; 3 new ones were added to tackle Tim's own chronic problems.
- The order of the questions was tested: light, concrete questions (the book you gift most, the item under 100 USD) go first so busy people can "warm up", and heavy questions (the billboard) come later.
- Each question has an "if it helps" sub-prompt (for example, permission to quote someone else on the billboard question) because that sub-prompt unlocks the answer.

## The 11 questions (briefly rephrased)
1. What is the book you have gifted most, or 1-3 books that changed you, and why?
2. What is the purchase of 100 USD or less that has most improved your life recently?
3. How has a failure (or apparent failure) set you up for later success? What is your "favorite failure"?
4. If you could put a giant billboard with a message in front of millions of people, what would it say and why? (Quoting someone else is allowed.)
5. What is the most worthwhile investment you have ever made (money, time, energy)?
6. What is an odd habit or "irrational" thing you love?
7. In the last 5 years, what new belief, behavior or habit has improved your life the most?
8. What advice would you give a smart, ambitious student about to enter the world? And what advice should they ignore?
9. What bad advice do you hear often in your field?
10. In the last 5 years, what have you learned to say no to? What did you realize, and what method did you use?
11. When you feel overwhelmed or unfocused, what do you do? What question do you ask yourself?

## Why Tim thinks each question works (summary)
- "Book you gift most" is easier to recall and lower-risk than "favorite book", and it is useful to many people.
- The failure question matters to Tim: successful people have not run out of weaknesses; they find their own strengths and build habits around them; everyone is fighting a battle others cannot see.
- The odd-habit question shows that everyone has their own "crazy" taste, so you are not alone.
- Questions 8 and 9, with their "what to ignore" part, are the important part: we often ask "what should I do" and rarely "what should I not do", yet what we do not do determines what we can do. Focus is often solved by identifying what to ignore.
- Question 10: saying "yes" is easy; saying "no" is the hard part.
- Question 11: if your mind is "frozen", nothing else matters as much as unjamming it first.

## A few framing ideas from the introduction
- A clear question gets a clear answer; a vague question gets mush. Tim quotes John Dewey: a problem well put is half solved.
- Tim's estimate for the book: out of ~140 profiles, you will like ~70, love ~35, and have your life changed by ~17; and the profile you skip today may be the one you need later.
- The author sees life as roughly 25% "finding yourself" and 75% "creating yourself".
- The Anaïs Nin line Tim keeps up at home: life shrinks or expands in proportion to one's courage.
- The book is not meant for passive reading; it is a call to action.
- Books that recur (as Tim notes): Man's Search for Meaning (Frankl), The Rational Optimist (Ridley), The Better Angels of Our Nature (Pinker), Sapiens (Harari), Poor Charlie's Almanack (Munger).

## Two sample answers Tim gave participants
- Amelia Boone (endurance athlete), for the "most worthwhile investment" question: she spent 450 USD to enter her first Toughest Mudder race in 2011 while still carrying law-school debt, finished among the 11 who completed it out of about 1,000, and it became the turning point of her career.
- Cheryl Strayed (author of Wild), for the odd-habit question: every bite of a sandwich must taste like the last, so she takes it apart and rearranges the filling so it is even.
