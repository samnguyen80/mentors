# Richard Feynman (1918-1988): Research on Major Decisions

> Research date: 2026-04-04
> Research goal: Feynman's major decisions, turning points, and controversial behavior across his life
> Note: [Fact] marks verified information; [Inference] marks reasonable speculation based on facts

---

## 1. Joining the Manhattan Project (1943, age 25)

**Event**: Bob Wilson found Feynman at Princeton and told him about a secret project: separating uranium isotopes to build an atomic bomb.

**Decision**: Hesitated at first, then decided to join.

**Decision logic**:
- [Fact] The core motive was fear that Germany would build the atomic bomb first. Feynman later said explicitly that what drove him was "fear that Nazi Germany would get nuclear weapons first"
- [Fact] Bob Wilson's persuasion strategy was clever: he said, "I know that once you understand what this project is doing, you will realize you have to take part"
- [Inference] Feynman was then at an early stage of his academic career (just doing his PhD at Princeton). The project gathered the top physicists in the US, so for a young man it was both a mission and an opportunity

**Result / later impact**:
- [Fact] Feynman later expressed regret: after Germany surrendered, he did not re-examine his reasons for staying in the project. "The reason I had originally joined no longer existed, but I didn't think about it at all, and just carried on"
- [Fact] The brief euphoria after the Trinity nuclear test quickly turned into deep unease
- [Inference] This experience shaped Feynman's later obsession with "never stop thinking": he had personally experienced the danger of inertial thinking

## 2. The Death of His Wife Arline (June 1945)

**Event**: Feynman's first love and first wife, Arline Greenbaum, died of tuberculosis at only 25. This happened one month before the Trinity nuclear test.

**Decision**: Feynman handled grief in an extremely rational way: he went straight back to work, and did not cry for the first time until months later.

**Decision logic**:
- [Fact] In his own account, he did something "psychological" to himself: because reality mattered so much to him, he first needed to understand on a physiological level what was happening to Arline, and this process of rationalization blocked the emotional response
- [Fact] In October 1946 (16 months after her death), Feynman wrote a letter to Arline. The letter was sealed and not opened until after Feynman's death in 1988
- [Fact] The letter says: "I find it hard to understand in my mind what it means to love you after you are dead — but I still want to comfort and take care of you — and I want you to love me and care for me"
- [Fact] The letter ends: "P.S. Please excuse my not mailing this — but I don't know your new address."

**Result / later impact**:
- [Inference] Arline's death plus the moral shock of the Manhattan Project sent Feynman into a severe psychological low after the war (he said he was almost unable to work during his first years at Cornell)
- [Fact] Feynman later said Arline "stood in the way of his loving someone else": this deep emotional imprint lasted a long time
- [Inference] This unmailed letter may be one of the most moving love letters of the 20th century, showing the "intensely emotional side beneath a rational shell" in Feynman

## 3. Leaving Cornell and Choosing Caltech (around 1950)

**Event**: After the Manhattan Project Feynman taught at Cornell, but considered leaving.

**Decision**: Accepted Caltech's offer, and never left again for the rest of his life.

**Decision logic**:
- [Fact] In Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman!, Feynman says the direct trigger for leaving was this: while putting snow chains on his car in a blizzard in Ithaca, the thought of "wanting to live in a warm climate" took firm root in his head
- [Fact] A year's visit in Brazil also reinforced his preference for warm climates
- [Fact] Caltech's academic atmosphere suited him: Feynman said that whenever he walked across campus, colleagues would run up to tell him about the latest breakthroughs
- [Inference] On the surface Feynman moved "because of the weather," but the deeper reason may be that his time at Cornell overlapped with his psychological low (Arline's death, postwar confusion), and a completely new environment had the meaning of a psychological reset

**Result / later impact**:
- [Fact] Feynman later became extremely anxious about offers from other universities, and finally made a decision: **never again consider working anywhere else**, no matter how attractive the pay or terms
- [Inference] This "once and for all" decision pattern is very Feynman: rather than repeatedly burning energy on choices, close off the option once. It is highly consistent with Buffett's idea of "don't let people use up your time"

## 4. Developing Feynman Diagrams and the Path Integral (1948)

**Event**: In quantum electrodynamics (QED), Feynman developed two revolutionary tools: the path integral method and Feynman diagrams.

**Decision**: Chose a methodological path completely different from the mainstream. The mainstream then was operator algebra (canonical quantization); Feynman took an intuitive, visual route.

**Decision logic**:
- [Fact] The core idea of the path integral: a particle going from A to B does not take one path but "all possible paths," and the final result is the superposition of all paths. The idea came from a hint of Dirac's, which Feynman developed into a complete mathematical framework
- [Fact] Feynman diagrams turned extremely tedious operator-algebra calculations into something where "drawing a picture on a napkin and applying rules" yields exact numerical predictions
- [Inference] Feynman's way of thinking was naturally visual and intuition-driven: he disliked abstract formalism and preferred methods where "I can see what is happening." This is of a piece with his later philosophy that "if you can't explain it simply, you haven't really understood it"

**Result / later impact**:
- [Fact] Feynman diagrams remain a basic tool of particle physicists today
- [Fact] The path integral method was later shown to be more elegant than traditional methods in many fields, including quantum field theory, statistical mechanics, and condensed matter physics
- [Fact] In 1965, Feynman shared the Nobel Prize in Physics with Schwinger and Tomonaga: the three obtained the same results by completely different methods
- [Inference] The success of Feynman diagrams shows that sometimes the most powerful innovation is not discovering new facts but inventing a new "way of looking at the problem"

## 5. Wanting to Refuse the Nobel Prize (1965)

**Event**: Feynman won the 1965 Nobel Prize in Physics for his QED work.

**Decision**: Seriously considered refusing, and in the end accepted.

**Decision logic**:
- [Fact] Feynman's attitude toward the Nobel Prize was always ambivalent. He thought "the Nobel committee should ask your consent before awarding the prize"
- [Fact] One reason he didn't want to accept involved Lewis Strauss: Strauss had stripped Oppenheimer of his security clearance, Feynman was angry about it, and did not want to accept an honor connected with the power establishment
- [Fact] Isidor Rabi advised him: "You should never turn a man's generosity as a sword against him"
- [Fact] The actual reason he finally accepted: he found that **refusing the Nobel Prize would draw more attention and trouble than accepting it**

**Result / later impact**:
- [Fact] Feynman later said in his memoir that he was "almost afraid of winning," worrying that from then on he would never again do work worth noticing
- [Inference] This fear reflects Feynman's wariness of "identity fixation": he did not want to be defined by a label. It matches his pattern of refusing honorary degrees and all kinds of titles
- [Fact] Feynman refused honorary degrees, saying "I don't want other people spending my time on these meaningless things"

## 6. Teaching in Brazil and His Critique of Education (1952, 1963)

**Event**: Feynman spent a year in Brazil (around 1952), and in 1963 gave the talk "The Problem of Teaching Physics in Latin America" at the first Inter-American Conference on Physics Education in Rio.

**Decision**: At the conference he declared publicly that "no science is being taught in Brazil," directly criticizing the entire education system.

**Decision logic**:
- [Fact] Feynman found that students could answer textbook questions instantly, but could not answer at all when the question was phrased differently: what they had memorized was vocabulary and formulas, not understanding
- [Fact] He criticized it as a "self-propagating system: people pass exams, then teach others to pass exams, but nobody actually understands anything"
- [Fact] He found that the "experimental data" in the textbooks were fabricated: the numbers looked like experimental results (with small deviations) but were in fact made up
- [Inference] Feynman's choice to criticize publicly rather than advise privately reflects his core belief: the duty to truth outranks social etiquette

**Result / later impact**:
- [Fact] This experience later spread widely through Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman! and became a classic case in the criticism of education
- [Inference] Feynman's loathing of "rote memorization disguised as understanding" directly shaped how he later taught at Caltech: the famous The Feynman Lectures on Physics was the result of his attempt to teach physics afresh from the ground up

## 7. The Challenger Accident Investigation (1986)

**Event**: On January 28, 1986, the Space Shuttle Challenger exploded, killing seven astronauts. NASA formed the Rogers Commission to investigate, and Feynman was one of its members.

**Decision**: Feynman chose to investigate independently, not bound by the commission's bureaucratic procedures; in a televised hearing he performed the famous live experiment with a glass of ice water and a piece of O-ring rubber.

**Decision logic**:
- [Fact] Feynman found that engineers at Morton Thiokol had warned NASA management the day before the launch that the O-ring seal would fail in cold conditions. But management ignored the engineers' opinion
- [Fact] NASA management recorded the O-ring test results as a "safety factor of 3": but Feynman found this claim completely distorted the facts; they used the word "safe" to describe a part that was in fact flawed and unsafe
- [Fact] On live television Feynman put the O-ring rubber into ice water to show that it loses its elasticity at low temperature: this 10-second demonstration was more persuasive than hundreds of pages of reports
- [Inference] Feynman's choice of "demonstrating" over "arguing" fits his philosophy of knowing exactly: if you can't make people see it in a simple way, you haven't really understood the problem

**Result / later impact**:
- [Fact] Feynman's investigation report was almost not included in the Rogers Commission's formal report: he was asked to put it in an appendix. Feynman threatened to quit the commission if it was not included
- [Fact] At the end of the report Feynman wrote the famous line: "For a successful technology, reality must take precedence over public relations, for Nature cannot be fooled."
- [Inference] The Challenger investigation displayed several core features of Feynman's decision pattern: (1) independent thinking, not led along by the institution's line; (2) letting experiments speak, not authority; (3) willingness to offend people: he was in essence publicly accusing NASA management of using wordplay to cover up a safety hazard

## 8. Competition and Collaboration with Murray Gell-Mann

**Event**: Feynman and Gell-Mann were both at Caltech, and were two of the most outstanding theoretical physicists of the second half of the 20th century.

**Decision / behavior pattern**: Both collaborated and competed, in a complicated relationship.

**Key facts**:
- [Fact] In 1957-1958 the two collaborated to develop the V-A theory of the weak interaction (vector minus axial vector), while Sudarshan and Marshak independently found the same theory
- [Fact] Both came from New York, but their styles were opposite: Feynman deliberately kept a working-class accent, while Gell-Mann pursued perfect pronunciation of every foreign word
- [Fact] Gell-Mann was a famous pedant and a walking encyclopedia of languages and knowledge; Feynman was known for a fast, onomatopoeia-rich communication style that cared not at all who the audience was
- [Fact] The two would make "surprisingly childish remarks" when assessing each other
- [Fact] After the summer of 1958 the two basically stopped collaborating

**Inferred logic of the competition**:
- [Inference] This was a collision between two completely different "modes of genius": Gell-Mann was encyclopedic, systematic, and pursued perfect form; Feynman was intuition-driven, visual, and pursued understanding of the essence
- [Inference] Their rivalry may have been mutually constructive: having a top-tier rival nearby forced both to stay at their highest level

## 9. Later Years and His Attitude Toward Cancer (1978-1988)

**Event**: From 1978 Feynman fought cancer for 8 years, suffering in turn from liposarcoma and Waldenstrom's macroglobulinemia.

**Decision**: Kept teaching until 15 days before his death; finally chose to stop treatment.

**Decision logic**:
- [Fact] Feynman's attitude toward illness included refusing to prolong pointless suffering, and meeting everything he was going through in an unsentimental way
- [Fact] Feynman's death was not an accident: it was a complex medical process, and a conscious choice about "when to stop fighting"
- [Fact] Even as his body deteriorated, Feynman still observed reality with curiosity, including the decline of his own body
- [Fact] His last words: "I'd hate to die twice. It's so boring."

**Result / later impact**:
- [Inference] This last line perfectly condenses Feynman's whole character: humor in the face of death, a loathing of boredom (hinting at the long waiting of the dying process), and a detachment of "I've seen enough"
- [Inference] Feynman's attitude toward death matched his attitude toward physics: don't avoid reality, don't gloss it over with pretty words, face things as they are

## 10. Cracking Safes, Playing Bongo Drums, and Other "Neglecting His Real Work"

**Event**: Feynman's list of "side pursuits" is astonishing: cracking safes at Los Alamos, becoming expert at the bongo drums, learning to draw, sketching at strip clubs, learning Mayan hieroglyphs, and so on.

**Decision / behavior pattern**: Feynman never regarded these as "neglecting his real work"; he believed curiosity should have no boundaries.

**Key facts**:
- [Fact] At Los Alamos, out of pure curiosity Feynman discovered the weakness of the filing-cabinet locks, and then left anonymous notes in the safes holding top-secret documents, scaring everyone
- [Fact] Feynman was a professional-level bongo drummer, which became part of his public image
- [Fact] When a Swedish publisher asked him for a photo of him drumming in order to "make theoretical physics more human," Feynman snapped back: "my drumming has nothing to do with my theoretical physics," and called the idea insulting
- [Inference] This reply reveals a key stance of Feynman's: he was not "playing" an interesting scientist; drumming is drumming and physics is physics, and neither needs to serve the other

**Inferred deeper logic**:
- [Inference] Feynman's "neglecting his real work" actually reflects a cognitive strategy: staying active in completely different domains to keep his thinking from hardening. Cracking safes trains pattern recognition and systematic trial and error, which is not essentially different from doing physics
- [Inference] Feynman's famous line "I was born not knowing and have had only a little time to change that here and there" shows that he treated all learning as one and the same activity: curiosity has no high or low

## Overall Analysis: Feynman's Decision DNA

From the 10 key events above, several core patterns of Feynman's decision-making can be distilled:

### Pattern 1: The "reality first" principle
Present throughout, from the Manhattan Project to the Challenger investigation, Feynman always insisted: **what the facts are matters more than what you wish they were**. His line "Nature cannot be fooled" is not rhetoric, it is faith.

### Pattern 2: "Close off the option once and for all"
Never considering other schools after choosing Caltech, accepting the Nobel Prize because refusing was more trouble: Feynman was good at using one decisive decision to remove the burden of future decisions.

### Pattern 3: "Demonstration > argument"
The O-ring ice-water experiment, the Brazil teaching critique, Feynman diagrams: he proved again and again that if you can't make people "see" it, you haven't really solved the problem.

### Pattern 4: "Curiosity without boundaries"
Drumming, lock-picking, and drawing are not "relaxation"; they are different expressions of the same cognitive activity. Feynman refused to narrow his identity to "physicist."

### Pattern 5: "Anti-identity-fixation"
Refusing honorary degrees, wariness of the Nobel Prize, disliking being labeled: Feynman continually resisted any force that tried to pin him to a fixed position.
